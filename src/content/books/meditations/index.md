## Book I

1\. From my grandfather Verus I learned good character and the mastery of my temper.

2\. From the reputation and memory of my father, modesty and a manly character.

3\. From my mother, piety and generosity, and restraint not only from wrong actions but even from wrong thoughts. From her, too, I learned to live simply, in a way far removed from the habits of the rich.

4\. From my great-grandfather, I gained the good fortune of not attending the public schools and of having good teachers at home instead, along with the knowledge that a man should spend generously on such things.

5\. From my tutor I learned to take the side of neither the Greens nor the Blues, the rival factions at the chariot races in the Circus, and not to become a partisan of either the Parmularii or the Scutarii at the gladiatorial games, the fighters armed with the small round shield or those armed with the long oblong one. From him, too, I learned to endure hard work, to want little, to work with my own hands, to keep out of other people's affairs, and to be slow to listen to slander.

6\. From Diognetus I learned not to waste my energy on trivial things, and not to believe what wonder-workers and conjurers say about incantations, the driving out of spirits, and the like. He taught me not to breed quails for fighting, nor to give myself over passionately to pastimes of that kind, and to tolerate frank speech. Through him I became at home in philosophy, and I went to hear the lectures first of Bacchius, then of Tandasis and Marcianus. Through him, too, I wrote dialogues as a young man, and I came to want the plank bed, the animal skin, and all the other austerities that belong to the Greek philosophical discipline.

7\. From Rusticus I received the conviction that my character needed correction and discipline. From him I learned not to be drawn into the competitive vanity of the sophists, nor to write on speculative subjects, nor to deliver little moralizing speeches, nor to display myself as a man who practices rigorous self-discipline or who does good deeds for the sake of show. He taught me to keep away from rhetoric, poetry, and fine writing; not to walk around the house in my outdoor clothes, nor to indulge in other affectations of that sort; and to write my letters plainly, like the letter Rusticus wrote to my mother from Sinuessa. He taught me, with regard to those who had offended me in words or done me harm, to be easily appeased and reconciled as soon as they showed themselves ready to make peace. He taught me to read carefully, rather than resting content with a superficial understanding of a book, and not to agree hastily with people who talk too much. And I owe to him my acquaintance with the discourses of Epictetus, which he shared with me from his own collection.

8\. From Apollonius I learned independence of mind and unwavering steadiness of purpose; to look to nothing but reason, not even for a moment; and to remain always the same, whether in sharp pain, at the loss of a child, or through a long illness. In him I saw clearly, in a living example, that the same man can be both utterly resolute and yielding, and that he need not be irritable when giving instruction. I had before my eyes a man who plainly regarded his experience and his skill in explaining philosophical principles as the least of his merits. And from him I learned how to accept what people consider favors from friends, without either being humbled by them or letting them pass without acknowledgment.

9\. From Sextus I gained a kindly disposition, the example of a household governed with a father's care, and the idea of living in accordance with nature. From him, too, I learned a gravity free of affectation, careful attention to the interests of my friends, and patience with the ignorant and with those who form opinions without reflection. He could adapt himself so readily to everyone that his company was more pleasing than any flattery, and yet those who associated with him held him in the highest reverence. He had the ability to discover and to arrange, intelligently and methodically, the principles necessary for life. He never showed anger or any other passion; he was entirely free of passion and at the same time deeply affectionate. He could express approval without fuss, and he possessed great learning without making a display of it.

10\. From Alexander the grammarian I learned to refrain from finding fault, and not to rebuke reproachfully anyone who used a crude, ungrammatical, or strange-sounding expression. Instead, he would tactfully introduce the very expression that ought to have been used, whether in the course of answering, or of confirming what had been said, or of joining in a discussion of the subject itself rather than the word, or by some other suitable hint.

11\. From Fronto I learned to recognize the envy, duplicity, and hypocrisy that belong to a tyrant, and that, in general, those among us who are called Patricians are somewhat lacking in fatherly affection.

12\. From Alexander the Platonist I learned not to say to anyone often, or without need, or to write in a letter, that I have no time; nor to keep excusing myself from the duties we owe to those we live among by pleading urgent business.

13\. From Catulus I learned not to shrug off a friend's criticism, even when it is unfounded, but to try to restore him to his usual frame of mind; to be ready to speak well of my teachers, as Domitius and Athenodotus are said to have done; and to love my children sincerely.

14\. From my brother Severus I learned to love my family, to love truth, and to love justice. Through him I came to know Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion, and Brutus, and from him I received the idea of a state with one law for all, governed on the basis of equal rights and equal freedom of speech, and the idea of a monarchy that values above all else the freedom of the governed. I learned from him, as well, consistency and unwavering constancy in my devotion to philosophy, together with a disposition to do good, to give readily to others, to cherish good hopes, and to trust that my friends love me. I observed that he made no secret of his opinion of those he condemned, and that his friends never had to guess what he wanted or did not want, because it was perfectly plain.

15\. From Maximus I learned self-mastery and not to be swayed by anything; cheerfulness in every circumstance, illness included; a just blend of gentleness and dignity in one's character; and to do the work set before me without complaint. I observed that everyone believed he thought exactly as he spoke, and that in everything he did he never had a bad intention. He never showed astonishment or surprise; he was never in a hurry, never procrastinated, and was never perplexed or downcast. He never laughed to hide his annoyance, nor, on the other hand, was he ever quick-tempered or suspicious. He made a habit of acts of kindness, was ready to forgive, and was entirely free of falsehood. He gave the impression of a man who could not be turned aside from what was right, rather than of one who had been corrected into it. I noticed, too, that no one could ever feel that Maximus looked down on him, or ever venture to think himself the better man. He also had the gift of a pleasant and agreeable humor.

16\. In my father I observed gentleness of temper and unshakable resolve in matters he had settled after proper deliberation; no vanity about the things people call honors; a love of hard work and perseverance; a readiness to listen to anyone who had something to propose for the common good; unwavering firmness in giving every man what he deserved; and a sense, drawn from experience, of when to act vigorously and when to ease off. I observed that he had overcome all passion for boys, and that he regarded himself as no more than any other citizen. He released his friends from any obligation to dine with him or to attend him when he went abroad, and those who had been kept from accompanying him by some pressing business always found him unchanged toward them. I observed, too, his habit of careful inquiry whenever a decision had to be made, and his persistence: he never cut an investigation short because he was satisfied with first appearances. He was disposed to keep his friends, neither tiring of them quickly nor being extravagant in his affection. He was content on every occasion, and cheerful. He looked far ahead and provided for the smallest matters without fuss. He immediately checked popular applause and every kind of flattery. He kept constant watch over whatever was needed for the administration of the empire, managed its expenditure carefully, and patiently endured the criticism this earned him.

He was not superstitious toward the gods, nor did he court men's favor with gifts, or by trying to please them, or by flattering the crowd; he showed sobriety and steadiness in all things, and never a mean thought or action, nor any appetite for novelty. The comforts of life, which fortune supplied him in abundance, he used without arrogance and without apology, so that when he had them he enjoyed them unaffectedly, and when he did not have them he did not miss them. No one could ever have called him a sophist, a flippant household slave, or a pedant; everyone acknowledged him to be a mature and complete man, above flattery, and capable of managing both his own affairs and those of others. Beyond this, he honored those who were true philosophers, and while he did not reproach those who merely pretended to be philosophers, neither was he easily led by them. He was easy in conversation and made himself agreeable without any tiresome affectation. He took reasonable care of his health, not as someone greatly attached to life, nor out of concern for his appearance, nor yet carelessly, but in such a way that, thanks to his own attention, he very seldom needed a physician, or medicines, or ointments and poultices.

He was quick to give way, without envy, to those who had some particular gift, whether for eloquence, or for knowledge of the law or of ethics, or of anything else, and he helped them so that each might enjoy the reputation he deserved. He always acted in keeping with the institutions of his country, without making a show of doing so. Further, he disliked change and restlessness; he preferred to stay in the same places and to occupy himself with the same things. After his attacks of headache he would return at once, fresh and vigorous, to his usual work. His secrets were few, very few and very rare, and those concerned only public affairs. He showed prudence and economy in staging public spectacles, in constructing public buildings, in his donations to the people, and in similar matters, because he looked to what ought to be done, not to the reputation a man gains from his deeds. He did not bathe at odd hours; he had no taste for building houses, and he was indifferent to what he ate, to the texture and color of his clothes, and to the beauty of his slaves. His clothing came from Lorium, his villa on the coast, and generally from Lanuvium. We know how he treated the toll collector at Tusculum who begged his pardon, and that was how he behaved in everything.

There was nothing harsh in him, nothing implacable or violent, nothing, as one might say, pushed to the point of breaking a sweat; he examined everything in turn, as though he had all the time in the world, calmly, in an orderly way, vigorously and consistently. What Xenophon records of Socrates might be said of him as well: that he could both abstain from and enjoy those things that most people are too weak to abstain from and cannot enjoy without excess. To be strong enough to do without the one and to remain temperate in the other is the mark of a man whose soul is complete and unconquerable, as he showed during the illness of Maximus.

17\. To the gods I owe it that I had good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good companions, and good relatives and friends: nearly everything good. I owe it to the gods, too, that I was never rushed into offending any of them, even though I had a disposition that might have led me to do something of the kind had the opportunity arisen; by their favor, circumstances never combined to put me to that test. I am grateful to the gods that I was not raised any longer with my grandfather's concubine; that I preserved the flower of my youth, and did not prove my manhood before the proper time, but even put it off; and that I was placed under a ruler and father who was able to strip all pride from me and to make me understand that a man can live in a palace without needing bodyguards or embroidered robes, or torches and statues and similar display. Such a man, I came to see, has it in his power to bring himself very close to the manner of a private citizen without becoming any meaner in his thinking, or any more negligent in carrying out, as befits a ruler, the duties that the public interest requires.

I thank the gods for giving me a brother who could rouse me, by his moral character, to keep watch over myself, and who at the same time gladdened me with his respect and affection. I thank them that my children have been neither dull-witted nor deformed in body; that I did not make greater progress in rhetoric, poetry, and the other studies, in which I might well have become wholly absorbed had I seen myself advancing; that I lost no time in raising those who had brought me up to the positions of honor they seemed to want, rather than putting them off with the hope that I would do it later, on the grounds that they were still young; and that I knew Apollonius, Rusticus, and Maximus. I thank them that I received clear and frequent impressions of what it means to live according to nature, and of what kind of life that is, so that, as far as the gods, their gifts, their help, and their inspirations were concerned, nothing prevented me from living according to nature at once, although I still fall short of it through my own fault, and through not heeding the reminders of the gods and, I might almost say, their direct instructions.

I am grateful that my body has held out so long under such a life; that I never touched either Benedicta or Theodotus, and that, when I did later fall prey to the passions of love, I was cured of them; that although I was often out of temper with Rusticus, I never did anything I had cause to regret; and that although my mother was fated to die young, she spent the last years of her life with me. I am grateful that whenever I wished to help someone in need, or on any other occasion, I was never told that I lacked the means to do it, and that I myself never fell into the same necessity of having to accept anything from another; that I have such a wife, so obedient, so affectionate, and so unaffected; that I had plenty of good teachers for my children; and that remedies were shown to me in dreams, both for other ailments and against the spitting of blood and dizziness . . . And I am grateful that when I developed a taste for philosophy, I did not fall into the hands of any sophist, nor waste my time on writers of histories, or on solving syllogisms, or on studying phenomena in the heavens. For all these things require the help of the gods and of fortune.

Among the Quadi, on the Granua.

## Book II

1\. Begin each morning by telling yourself: Today I shall meet the meddler, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious, and the unsociable. All of this happens to them because they do not know what is good and what is evil. But I have seen that the nature of the good is beautiful and the nature of the bad is ugly, and that the nature of the wrongdoer himself is akin to mine, not merely because we share the same blood or seed, but because he shares in the same intelligence and the same portion of the divine. And so I cannot be harmed by any of them, for no one can fasten on me what is ugly; nor can I be angry with my kinsman, or hate him. We were made to work together, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. To work against one another, then, is contrary to nature, and to be resentful toward someone and turn away from him is to work against him.

2\. Whatever it is that I am, it is a little flesh, a little breath, and the ruling part. Put away your books; distract yourself no longer, for that is not permitted. Instead, as though you were already dying, despise the flesh: it is blood and bones and a mesh, a weave of nerves, veins, and arteries. Look at the breath, too, and see what sort of thing it is: air, and never the same air, but at every moment breathed out and drawn in again. The third, then, is the ruling part. Reflect on this: you are an old man. Do not let this part be a slave any longer, nor let it be jerked by strings like a puppet into unsocial movements, nor let it be discontented with your present lot or shrink from the future.

3\. Everything that comes from the gods is full of providence. What comes from fortune is not separate from nature, nor is it free of the interweaving and entanglement of the things ordered by providence. From there all things flow; and in addition there is necessity, and there is what benefits the whole universe, of which you are a part. Whatever the nature of the whole brings, and whatever helps to sustain that nature, is good for every part of nature. And the universe is preserved by the changes of the elements and equally by the changes of the things compounded from them. Let these principles be enough for you; hold them always as settled convictions. But cast away your thirst for books, so that you may die not grumbling, but cheerfully, sincerely, and grateful to the gods from the bottom of your heart.

4\. Remember how long you have been putting these things off, and how many times the gods have given you an opportunity that you have failed to use. You must now at last understand what universe you are part of, and from what governor of that universe your existence flows. You must understand, too, that a limit of time has been set for you, and that if you do not use it to clear the clouds from your mind, it will pass, and you will pass with it, and it will never return.

5\. At every moment, set your mind firmly, as a Roman and as a man, on doing what you have in hand with perfect and unaffected dignity, with affection, freedom, and justice, and give yourself relief from every other thought. You will give yourself that relief if you perform every act of your life as though it were your last, setting aside all carelessness and all emotional resistance to the commands of reason, all hypocrisy, all self-love, and all discontent with the portion you have been given. You see how few things a man must grasp in order to live a life that flows quietly and resembles the existence of the gods; for the gods, for their part, will ask nothing more of anyone who keeps to these things.

6\. Do wrong to yourself, do wrong to yourself, my soul; but soon you will no longer have the chance to honor yourself. Each person's life is sufficient. Yours, however, is nearly finished, and still your soul does not respect itself, but places your happiness in the souls of others.

7\. Are you distracted by the external things that befall you? Give yourself time to learn something new and good, and stop being spun in circles. But then you must also guard against being swept off in the opposite direction. Those people are triflers too who have exhausted themselves in life with activity, yet have no aim toward which to direct every impulse and, in short, every thought.

8\. Rarely has anyone been seen to be unhappy because he failed to notice what was happening in another person's mind; but those who do not attend to the movements of their own minds are bound to be unhappy.

9\. Always keep this in mind: what the nature of the whole is, and what my own nature is, and how the one is related to the other, and what kind of part it is of what kind of whole. Remember, too, that no one can prevent you from always doing and saying what accords with the nature of which you are a part.

10\. Theophrastus, in comparing wrongful acts (the sort of comparison one might make according to the common notions of mankind), says, like a true philosopher, that offenses committed through desire deserve more blame than those committed through anger. The person stirred by anger seems to turn away from reason with a certain pain and an involuntary inner contraction; but the one who offends through desire, overpowered by pleasure, seems somehow more self-indulgent and more effeminate in his wrongdoing. Rightly, then, and in a manner worthy of philosophy, he said that an offense committed with pleasure is more blameworthy than one committed with pain. On the whole, the angry man resembles someone who has first been wronged and is driven by pain into anger, whereas the other is moved by his own impulse to do wrong, carried toward the act by desire.

11\. Since you may depart from life at this very moment, govern every act and thought accordingly. But leaving the company of men is nothing to fear if there are gods, for they will not involve you in evil. And if they do not exist, or if they take no interest in human affairs, what is it to me to live in a universe empty of gods or empty of providence? In truth, though, they do exist, and they do care for human affairs, and they have placed entirely within a person's power the means of not falling into what is truly evil. As for everything else, if any of it were evil, they would have provided for this as well, so that it would lie wholly within a person's power not to fall into it. Now, how can something that does not make a person worse make that person's life worse? The nature of the universe could not have overlooked these things, whether through ignorance, or through having the knowledge but lacking the power to guard against them or correct them. Nor could it have made so great a mistake, through want of power or want of skill, as to let good and evil befall the good and the bad indiscriminately. Yet death, certainly, and life, honor and dishonor, pain and pleasure, all happen equally to good people and bad, and they make us neither better nor worse. Therefore they are neither good nor evil.

12\. How quickly everything vanishes: in the universe, the bodies themselves; in time, the memory of them. What is the nature of all things perceived by the senses, especially those that lure us with the bait of pleasure, or frighten us with pain, or are trumpeted abroad by vaporous fame? How worthless, contemptible, sordid, perishable, and dead they are: all this it is the task of the intellect to observe. The intellect must also observe who these people are whose opinions and voices confer reputation, and what death is. If a person looks at death in itself, and through the abstracting power of reflection breaks down into their parts all the images the imagination attaches to it, he will regard it as nothing but an operation of nature; and anyone who is afraid of an operation of nature is a child. Death, however, is not only an operation of nature but also something that serves nature's purposes. The intellect must observe, finally, how a human being draws near to God, by which part of himself, and when that part is so disposed.

13\. Nothing is more wretched than a man who roams over everything in a circle, who, as the poet Pindar says, "pries into the things beneath the earth," and tries to guess what is in his neighbors' minds, without realizing that it is enough to attend to the divine spirit within himself and to revere it sincerely. Reverence for this spirit consists in keeping it pure from passion and thoughtlessness, and from discontent with whatever comes from gods and men. What comes from the gods deserves veneration for its excellence. What comes from men should be dear to us because of our kinship with them, and sometimes, in a way, it even moves our pity because of their ignorance of good and bad, a defect no less serious than the one that robs us of the power to tell white from black.

14\. Even if you were to live three thousand years, or three thousand times ten thousand years, remember nonetheless that no one loses any life other than the one he is now living, nor lives any life other than the one he is now losing. So the longest life and the shortest come to the same thing. The present moment is the same for everyone, though what passes away is not the same; and so what is lost turns out to be a mere instant. No one can lose either the past or the future, for how could anyone take from a person what he does not have? Keep these two things in mind, then. The first is that all things from eternity have been of like forms and come round in a cycle, so that it makes no difference whether a person sees the same things for a hundred years, or two hundred, or for an infinite time. The second is that the longest-lived and the one who will die soonest lose exactly the same. For the present is the only thing of which a person can be deprived, if it is true that this is the only thing he has, and that no one can lose what he does not have.

15\. Remember that everything is opinion. The saying of the Cynic Monimus is plain enough, and plain too is its usefulness, if one takes from it what can be taken, so far as it is true.

16\. The human soul does violence to itself, first of all, when it becomes, so far as it is able, an abscess, a kind of tumor on the universe. To be vexed at anything that happens is to separate ourselves from nature, in some part of which the natures of all other things are contained. Second, the soul does violence to itself when it turns away from any person, or even moves against him intending harm, as the souls of angry people do. Third, it does violence to itself when it is overpowered by pleasure or by pain. Fourth, when it plays a part, and does or says anything insincerely and falsely. Fifth, when it lets any of its acts or movements go without an aim, and does anything thoughtlessly, without considering what it is. Even the smallest things ought to be done with reference to an end, and the end of rational creatures is to follow the reason and the law of the most ancient city and commonwealth.

17\. In human life, time is a point, substance is in flux, perception is dim, the whole body's composition is subject to decay, the soul is a whirl, fortune is hard to read, and fame is a thing without judgment. To put it all in a word: everything that belongs to the body is a stream, and everything that belongs to the soul is a dream and a vapor; life is a war and a stranger's stay in a foreign land, and after fame comes oblivion. What, then, can guide a person? One thing, and one thing only: philosophy. Philosophy consists in keeping the divine spirit within free from violence and unharmed, superior to pains and pleasures, doing nothing without purpose, nothing falsely or hypocritically, and feeling no need for anyone else to do or refrain from doing anything. It consists, besides, in accepting all that happens and all that is allotted as coming from the same source, wherever it may be, from which he himself came. And it consists, finally, in awaiting death with a cheerful mind, as nothing more than the dissolution of the elements of which every living being is composed. If the elements themselves suffer no harm in continually changing each into another, why should anyone fear the change and dissolution of all of them? It is in accordance with nature, and nothing that accords with nature is evil.

Written at Carnuntum.

## Book III

1\. We ought to consider not only that our life is wasting away day by day, with a smaller portion of it left, but also something else. If a person lives longer, it is quite uncertain whether his understanding will remain adequate to grasp things, and whether it will keep the contemplative power that strives to gain knowledge of the divine and the human. If he begins to slip into dotage, his perspiration and nutrition, his imagination and appetite, and every other faculty of that kind will not fail. But the power to make proper use of ourselves, to fill up the full measure of our duty, to analyze every appearance clearly, to judge whether the time has come to depart from life, and everything else of the kind that absolutely demands a disciplined reason: all this is already extinguished. We must hurry, then, not only because each day brings us nearer to death, but also because our grasp and understanding of things are the first to fail.

2\. We ought also to notice that even the incidental byproducts of nature's works have something pleasing and attractive about them. When bread is baked, for instance, certain parts split open at the surface. These cracks, though they run contrary in a way to the baker's design, have a kind of beauty, and in their own peculiar way they stir the appetite. Figs, too, burst open when fully ripe, and in ripe olives their very nearness to decay lends the fruit a peculiar beauty. The ears of grain bending down, the lion's heavy brows, the foam flowing from the mouth of a wild boar, and many other things are far from beautiful if examined one by one. Nevertheless, because they follow from things formed by nature, they help to adorn those things and give pleasure to the mind. So if a person has a feeling for, and a deeper insight into, the things produced in the universe, there will scarcely be one of these incidental consequences that does not seem to him arranged, in its own way, to give pleasure. He will look upon the actual gaping jaws of wild beasts with no less pleasure than the imitations that painters and sculptors present. In an old woman or an old man he will be able to see a certain ripeness and grace, and he will be able to look upon the attractive loveliness of the young with chaste eyes. Many such things will present themselves, pleasing not to everyone but only to the person who has become truly familiar with Nature and her works.

3\. Hippocrates, after curing many diseases, himself fell sick and died. The Chaldaean astrologers foretold the deaths of many, and then fate caught them too. Alexander, Pompey, and Gaius Caesar, after so often razing whole cities and cutting down tens of thousands of cavalry and infantry in battle, at last departed from life themselves. Heraclitus, after all his speculations on the conflagration of the universe, had his insides filled with water and died smeared all over with mud. Lice destroyed Democritus, and lice of another sort killed Socrates. What does all this mean? You have embarked, you have made the voyage, you have reached the shore: get out. If it is to another life, there is no lack of gods even there. But if it is to a state without sensation, you will no longer be gripped by pains and pleasures, nor will you be a slave to the vessel of the body, which is as far inferior as the one who serves it is superior. For the servant is intelligence and divinity; the vessel is earth and corruption.

4\. Do not waste what remains of your life in thoughts about other people, unless you direct those thoughts toward some purpose of common benefit. While you are occupied with such thoughts, you lose the chance to do something else: thoughts such as, What is so-and-so doing, and why? What is he saying, what is he thinking, what is he plotting? Whatever else of this kind there is leads us to wander away from watching over our own ruling faculty. In the sequence of our thoughts, then, we should check everything aimless and useless, and above all every overcurious and malicious impulse. A person should train himself to think only of things such that, if someone suddenly asked him, "What are you thinking about right now?" he could answer at once, with perfect openness, "This" or "That." His words would then make it plain that everything in him is simple and kindly, fitting for a social creature, one who gives no thought at all to pleasure or sensual enjoyment and harbors no rivalry, envy, or suspicion, nor anything else he would blush to admit was in his mind. Such a person, who no longer puts off taking his place among the best, is like a priest and servant of the gods. He makes use, too, of the divinity planted within him, which keeps him unstained by pleasure, unharmed by any pain, untouched by any insult, and insensible to any wrong. He is a fighter in the noblest of contests, one who cannot be overpowered by any passion, dyed deep with justice, welcoming with his whole soul everything that happens and is assigned to him as his portion. Only rarely, and never without great necessity and for the common good, does he imagine what someone else is saying, doing, or thinking. He makes only what belongs to himself the material of his activity, and he constantly reflects on what has been allotted to him out of the sum of all things; he makes his own acts honorable, and he is convinced that his own portion is good. For the lot assigned to each person is carried along with him and carries him along with it. He remembers, too, that every rational creature is his kinsman, and that to care for all people accords with human nature. Yet one should hold to the opinion not of everyone, but only of those who plainly live according to nature. As for those who do not, he always keeps in mind what sort of people they are, at home and abroad, by night and by day, and what they are, and with whom they share their impure lives. Accordingly, he sets no value whatever on praise from such people, since they are not even satisfied with themselves.

5\. Do not work unwillingly, nor without regard for the common interest, nor without due consideration, nor with a divided mind. Do not dress up your thoughts in studied ornament, and be neither a person of many words nor one busy with too many things. Further, let the divinity within you be the guardian of a living being who is manly and mature, engaged in public affairs, a Roman, and a ruler: one who has taken up his post like a man awaiting the signal that recalls him from life, ready to go, needing neither oath nor any man's testimony. Be cheerful as well, and do not seek outside help or the tranquillity that others provide. A person must stand upright, not be held upright by others.

6\. Suppose you find in human life anything better than justice, truth, temperance, and courage: anything, in short, better than your own mind's contentment with the actions it enables you to perform in accordance with right reason, and with the circumstances assigned to you without your own choosing. If, I say, you see anything better than this, turn to it with all your soul and enjoy what you have found to be best. But suppose nothing appears better than the divinity planted within you, which has brought all your appetites under its control, which carefully examines every impression, which, as Socrates said, has detached itself from the persuasions of the senses, submitted itself to the gods, and cares for humankind. If you find everything else smaller and less valuable than this, then give room to nothing else. For once you turn aside and lean toward something else, you will no longer be able, without distraction, to give first place to that good which is properly yours and your own. It is not right that anything of a different kind, such as the praise of the many, or power, or the enjoyment of pleasure, should compete with what is rationally and politically (or practically) good. All these things, even if they seem to fit in with the better things to a slight degree, suddenly gain the upper hand and carry us off. But you, I say, should simply and freely choose the better, and hold fast to it. "But what is useful is the better." Very well: if it is useful to you as a rational being, keep to it; but if it is useful to you only as an animal, say so, and maintain your judgment without arrogance. Only make sure that you conduct the inquiry by a sound method.

7\. Never value anything as being to your advantage if it will force you to break your word, lose your self-respect, hate anyone, suspect, curse, play the hypocrite, or desire anything that needs walls and curtains to hide it. A person who has preferred above everything else his own intelligence and guardian spirit, and the worship of its excellence, plays no tragic role, utters no groans, and needs neither solitude nor a crowd. Most important of all, he will live neither pursuing death nor fleeing from it. Whether his soul is to remain enclosed in his body for a longer or a shorter time does not concern him in the least. Even if he must depart at once, he will go as readily as if he were doing any other thing that can be done with decency and order. Throughout his life he takes care of this alone: that his thoughts never turn away from anything that befits an intelligent creature and a member of a civic community.

8\. In the mind of one who has been disciplined and purified you will find no corrupt matter, no impurity, no sore merely skinned over. Nor is his life left incomplete when fate overtakes him, as one might say of an actor who leaves the stage before the play is finished. There is nothing servile in him, nothing affected, nothing too closely bound to other things, and yet nothing cut off from them; nothing deserving of blame, nothing that seeks a hiding place.

9\. Revere the faculty that forms opinions. On this faculty it wholly depends whether your ruling part will harbor any opinion at odds with nature and with the constitution of a rational being. This faculty promises freedom from hasty judgment, friendship toward others, and obedience to the gods.

10\. Throw away everything else, then, and hold to these few things alone. Remember, besides, that each person lives only in this present moment, which is an indivisible point, and that all the rest of his life is either past or uncertain. Short, then, is the time each person lives, and small the corner of the earth where he lives. Short, too, is even the longest fame after death, and even that is carried on only by a succession of poor human beings who will very soon die themselves, and who do not know even themselves, much less someone long dead.

11\. To the aids already mentioned, add this one: make for yourself a definition or description of whatever presents itself to you, so that you see distinctly what kind of thing it is in its substance, stripped bare, as a complete whole. Tell yourself its proper name, and the names of the things from which it was compounded and into which it will be resolved. Nothing so elevates the mind as the ability to examine methodically and truthfully every object that meets you in life, and always to look at things so as to see at the same time what kind of universe this is, what use each thing serves in it, and what value each thing has in relation to the whole and in relation to man, who is a citizen of the highest city, of which all other cities are like households. A person should consider what each thing is, what it is composed of, and how long it is in the nature of this thing now making an impression on me to last, and what virtue I need in dealing with it, such as gentleness, courage, truthfulness, fidelity, simplicity, contentment, and the rest. So on every occasion one should say: This comes from God. This comes from the allotment and spinning of the thread of destiny, and from coincidence and chance of that kind. And this comes from someone of the same stock as myself, a kinsman and partner, who does not, however, know what accords with his nature. But I know, and so I treat him with kindness and justice, according to the natural law of fellowship. At the same time, in things that are morally indifferent, I try to determine the proper value of each.

12\. If you do the work before you, following right reason earnestly, energetically, and calmly, letting nothing else distract you, but keeping your divine part pure, as though you were bound to give it back at once; if you hold to this, expecting nothing and fearing nothing, content with your present activity in accordance with nature and with heroic truthfulness in every word and sound you utter, you will live happily. And no one can prevent this.

13\. As physicians always keep their instruments and knives at hand for cases that suddenly demand their skill, so you should keep your principles ready for understanding things divine and human, and for doing everything, even the smallest, mindful of the bond that unites the divine and the human. You will do nothing well that concerns human affairs without also referring it to the divine, nor the reverse.

14\. Wander aimlessly no longer. You will never read your own notebooks, nor the deeds of the ancient Romans and Greeks, nor the extracts from books that you were saving for your old age. Hurry, then, toward the end that lies before you, and, casting aside idle hopes, come to your own aid, if you care for yourself at all, while it is still in your power.

15\. People do not know how many things are meant by the words *stealing*, *sowing*, *buying*, *keeping quiet*, and *seeing what ought to be done*. None of this is accomplished by the eyes but by another kind of vision.

16\. Body, soul, intelligence: sensation belongs to the body, appetites to the soul, principles to the intelligence. To receive the impressions of forms through appearances belongs even to animals. To be jerked about by the strings of desire, like a puppet, belongs both to wild beasts and to men who have made themselves effeminate, and to a Phalaris or a Nero. And to have an intelligence that guides one toward whatever appears suitable belongs also to those who do not believe in the gods, who betray their country, and who commit their foul deeds once the doors are shut. If, then, everything else is shared by all those I have mentioned, what remains as peculiar to the good man is this: to be pleased and content with whatever happens, and with the thread that is spun for him; not to defile the divinity planted in his breast, nor disturb it with a crowd of images, but to keep it tranquil, following it obediently as a god, saying nothing contrary to truth and doing nothing contrary to justice. And if all men refuse to believe that he lives a simple, modest, and contented life, he is angry with none of them, nor does he stray from the road that leads to the end of life, which a man should reach pure, tranquil, ready to depart, and perfectly reconciled to his lot without any compulsion.

## Book IV

1\. When the ruling power within us is in accord with nature, it meets events in such a way that it always adapts easily to whatever is possible and whatever is presented to it. It requires no particular material to work with. It moves toward its aims, though under certain conditions, and it makes material for itself out of whatever opposes it. It behaves like a fire that seizes whatever falls into it. A small flame would be smothered by such things, but a strong fire quickly takes possession of the fuel heaped upon it, consumes it, and rises higher by means of that very fuel.

2\. Let no act be done without a purpose, nor in any way other than according to the perfect principles of the art.

3\. People seek retreats for themselves: houses in the country, the seashore, the mountains. You, too, are inclined to long for such things intensely. But this is entirely the mark of the most ordinary sort of person, for it is in your power, whenever you choose, to retreat into yourself. Nowhere does a man retreat with more quiet or more freedom from trouble than into his own soul, especially when he holds within him thoughts of such a kind that by looking into them he is at once in perfect tranquility; and I maintain that tranquility is nothing other than the good ordering of the mind. Give yourself this retreat constantly, then, and renew yourself. Let your principles be brief and fundamental, so that as soon as you return to them they will be enough to cleanse the soul completely and send you back free of all discontent with the things to which you are returning.

For what are you discontented with? The wickedness of men? Call to mind this conclusion: that rational creatures exist for one another, that patience is a part of justice, and that people do wrong involuntarily. Consider how many, after mutual enmity, suspicion, hatred, and fighting, have been stretched out dead and reduced to ashes, and be quiet at last. But perhaps you are dissatisfied with the portion assigned to you by the universe. Then recall the alternative: either there is providence, or there are only atoms, a random collision of things. Or remember the arguments proving that the world is a kind of political community, and be quiet at last. But perhaps bodily things will still lay hold of you. Then consider further that the mind, once it has drawn itself apart and discovered its own power, does not mingle with the breath of life, whether that breath moves gently or violently; and think, too, of everything you have heard and agreed to about pain and pleasure, and be quiet at last. But perhaps the craving for what is called fame will torment you. Then see how quickly everything is forgotten; look at the chaos of infinite time stretching on either side of the present, at the emptiness of applause, at the fickleness and poor judgment of those who presume to hand out praise, and at the narrowness of the space within which all of it is confined, and be quiet at last. For the whole earth is a point, and how small a corner of it is the place where you live, how few people are in it, and what sort of people they are who will praise you.

What remains, then, is this. Remember to retreat into this little territory of your own. Above all, do not distract or strain yourself, but be free, and look at things as a man, as a human being, as a citizen, as a mortal. And among the thoughts readiest to hand, the ones you turn to first, let there be these two. The first is that things do not touch the soul, for they are external and stand motionless; our disturbances come only from the opinion within us. The second is that everything you see is about to change and will soon be no more; and keep constantly in mind how many such changes you have already witnessed. The universe is transformation; life is opinion.

4\. If the intellectual part of us is common to all, then reason, in virtue of which we are rational beings, is also common. If so, then the reason that commands us what to do and what not to do is common as well. If so, there is also a common law. If so, we are fellow citizens. If so, we are members of a political community. If so, the world is, in a sense, a single state. For of what other common political community could anyone say the whole human race are members? And it is from this common political community that our very intellectual faculty, our reasoning faculty, and our capacity for law are derived; or where else could they come from? For just as my earthly part is a portion given to me from some earth, and my watery part from another element, and my hot and fiery part from some particular source of its own (for nothing comes out of nothing, just as nothing returns into nothing), so the intellectual part, too, comes from some source.

5\. Death, like birth, is a mystery of nature: a combination out of the same elements, and a dissolution back into them. It is not at all a thing of which anyone should be ashamed, for it is not contrary to the nature of a rational creature, nor contrary to the reason of our constitution.

6\. It is natural that such things should be done by such people; it is a matter of necessity. Whoever refuses to accept this would refuse to let the fig tree have its sap. But in any case, keep this in mind: within a very short time both you and he will be dead, and soon not even your names will remain.

7\. Take away your opinion, and the complaint "I have been harmed" is taken away. Take away the complaint "I have been harmed," and the harm itself is taken away.

8\. What does not make a man worse than he was does not make his life worse either, nor does it harm him from without or from within.

9\. The nature of what is universally useful has been compelled to do this.

10\. Consider that everything that happens, happens justly, and if you observe carefully you will find it so. I do not mean only in the sense of an unbroken chain of cause and effect, but in the sense of justice, as though it were done by one who assigns to each thing its due worth. Keep observing, then, as you have begun; and whatever you do, do it with this in view: being good, in the sense in which a man is properly understood to be good. Hold to this in every action.

11\. Do not take the view of things held by the man who wrongs you, or the view he wants you to take, but see them as they truly are.

12\. A man should always keep these two rules ready. The first is to do only what the reason of the ruling and lawgiving faculty suggests for the benefit of mankind. The second is to change your opinion if someone is at hand to set you right and move you from it. But this change of opinion must come only from genuine conviction that something is just, or for the common good, or the like, and not because it seems pleasant or brings reputation.

13\. Do you have reason? I do. Then why don't you use it? For if reason is doing its own work, what more do you want?

14\. You have existed as a part. You will vanish into what produced you; or rather, you will be taken back by transformation into its generative principle, the seed from which all things grow.

15\. Many grains of incense on the same altar: one falls sooner, another later, but it makes no difference.

16\. Within ten days you will seem a god to the very people who now regard you as a beast and an ape, if you return to your principles and to the worship of reason.

17\. Do not act as though you were going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you live, while it is in your power, be good.

18\. How much trouble a man spares himself when he does not look to see what his neighbor says or does or thinks, but only to what he himself does, that it may be just and pure. Or, as Agathon says, do not look around at the depraved morals of others, but run straight along the line without swerving from it.

19\. The man who passionately desires fame after death does not consider that every one of those who remember him will also die very soon, and then those who succeed them in turn, until the whole memory of him has been snuffed out as it passes through a succession of people who foolishly admire and then perish. But suppose that those who remember him were immortal, and the memory immortal too: what is that to you? I do not ask what it is to the dead, but what it is to the living. What is praise, except insofar as it has some practical use? For you are now rejecting, at the wrong moment, the gift of nature, clinging to something else...

20\. Everything that is in any way beautiful is beautiful in itself and complete in itself; praise forms no part of it. A thing is made neither worse nor better by being praised. I say this even of the things that ordinary people call beautiful, such as material objects and works of art. What is truly beautiful needs nothing beyond itself, any more than law does, or truth, or kindness, or modesty. Which of these is beautiful because it is praised, or spoiled because it is blamed? Is an emerald made worse than it was if no one praises it? Or gold, ivory, purple dye, a lyre, a small knife, a flower, a shrub?

21\. If souls continue to exist, how has the air made room for them from all eternity? But then, how does the earth make room for the bodies of those buried over so many ages? Just as, on earth, these bodies, after lasting for some period, whatever it may be, change and decompose and so make room for other corpses, so the souls that pass into the air, after persisting for a time, are transformed and dispersed. They take on a fiery nature by being absorbed into the generative intelligence of the universe, and in this way they make room for the new souls that come to dwell there. This is the answer a man might give on the assumption that souls continue to exist. But we must think not only of the number of bodies buried in this way, but also of the number of animals eaten every day by us and by other creatures. What a multitude is consumed, and thus in a sense buried in the bodies of those that feed on them! And yet the earth has room for them, because they are changed into blood and transformed into the element of air or of fire.

How is the truth of this matter to be investigated? By distinguishing between what is material and what is the cause of form.

22\. Do not be spun about, but in every impulse keep justice in view, and with every impression preserve your power of understanding.

23\. Everything that is in harmony with you, O Universe, is in harmony with me. Nothing that comes at the right time for you is too early or too late for me. Everything your seasons bring is fruit to me, O Nature. From you are all things, in you are all things, to you all things return. The poet says of Athens, "Dear city of Cecrops," after its legendary first king; will you not say of the world, "Dear city of Zeus"?

24\. "Occupy yourself with few things," says the philosopher, "if you would be tranquil." But consider whether it would be better to say: do what is necessary, and whatever the reason of a naturally social creature requires, and in the way it requires. This brings not only the tranquility that comes from doing well but also the tranquility that comes from doing little. For since most of what we say and do is unnecessary, a man who cuts it away will have more leisure and less anxiety. On every occasion, therefore, a man should ask himself: is this one of the unnecessary things? And he should cut away not only unnecessary actions but also unnecessary thoughts, for then superfluous actions will not follow.

25\. Try how the life of the good man suits you: the life of one who is satisfied with his portion of the whole, and content with his own just actions and kindly disposition.

26\. Have you seen those things? Look at these as well. Do not trouble yourself. Make yourself entirely simple. Does someone do wrong? He does the wrong to himself. Has something happened to you? Very well: everything that happens was apportioned to you from the universe and spun out for you from the beginning. In short, your life is brief. You must make the most of the present with the help of reason and justice. Be sober even in your relaxation.

27\. Either the universe is an ordered whole, or it is a chaos jumbled together, but still a universe. Yet can there be a certain order within you and disorder in the whole? And this when all things are so distinct yet so intermingled and so bound together in mutual sympathy?

28\. A black character, an effeminate character, a stubborn character: bestial, childish, brutish, stupid, counterfeit, scurrilous, fraudulent, tyrannical.

29\. If the man who does not know what is in the universe is a stranger to it, no less a stranger is the man who does not know what is happening in it. He is a runaway who flees from social reason. He is blind who shuts the eyes of the understanding. He is poor who needs another and does not have from himself everything useful for life. He is a tumor on the universe who withdraws and cuts himself off from the reason of our common nature because he is displeased with what happens, for the same nature that produces these events also produced him. He is a fragment torn from the state who tears his own soul away from the soul of rational creatures, which is one.

30\. One man is a philosopher without a tunic, another without a book; here is another half naked. "I have no bread," he says, "and yet I hold fast to reason." And I get no living from my learning, and yet I hold fast to reason.

31\. Love the craft you have learned, however humble, and be content with it. Pass through the rest of your life as one who has entrusted everything he has to the gods with his whole soul, making yourself neither the tyrant nor the slave of any man.

32\. Consider, for example, the times of Vespasian. You will see all the same things: people marrying, raising children, falling sick, dying, waging war, feasting, trading, farming, flattering, stubbornly arrogant, suspicious, scheming, wishing for others to die, grumbling about the present, loving, hoarding wealth, coveting the consulship or a throne. Well, that life of theirs no longer exists anywhere. Move on again to the times of Trajan. Again, everything is the same, and their life too is gone. Look in the same way at the other eras of time and of whole nations, and see how many, after great exertions, soon fell and were dissolved into the elements. But above all you should think of those you yourself have known who wore themselves out over trifles, neglecting to do what accorded with their proper constitution, to hold fast to it, and to be content with it. And here it is necessary to remember that the attention given to each thing has its own proper value and proportion. In this way you will not grow discouraged, so long as you devote yourself to lesser matters no more than is fitting.

33\. Words once in common use are now obsolete, and so, in a sense, are the names of those once celebrated: Camillus, Caeso, Volesus, Leonnatus, and, a little later, Scipio and Cato, then Augustus, then Hadrian and Antoninus as well. All things quickly fade and become mere legend, and soon total oblivion buries them. And I say this of those who shone in some extraordinary way. As for the rest, the moment they have drawn their last breath they are gone, and no one speaks of them. And, to put an end to the matter, what is even everlasting remembrance? Nothing at all. What, then, deserves our serious effort? Only this: just thoughts, social actions, words that never lie, and a disposition that gladly welcomes everything that happens as necessary, as familiar, as flowing from a principle and a source of the same kind as ourselves.

34\. Give yourself up willingly to Clotho, the Fate who spins the thread of life, and let her spin your thread into whatever she pleases.

35\. Everything lasts only a day, both the one who remembers and the one who is remembered.

36\. Observe constantly that all things come about through change, and accustom yourself to reflect that the nature of the universe loves nothing so much as to change what exists and make new things like them. For everything that exists is, in a sense, the seed of what will come from it. But you think only of seeds cast into the earth or into a womb, and this is a very crude notion.

37\. You will soon be dead, and you are not yet simple, nor free of disturbance, nor rid of the suspicion that external things can harm you, nor kindly disposed toward all, nor do you yet place wisdom solely in acting justly.

38\. Examine the ruling principles of men, even of the wise: what sort of things they avoid, and what sort they pursue.

39\. What is evil for you does not reside in another person's ruling principle, nor in any turn or change of your bodily covering. Where, then, is it? In that part of you which has the power of forming opinions about evils. Let this part form no such opinions, and all is well. And even if its nearest neighbor, the poor body, is cut, burned, or filled with pus and corruption, let the part that judges these things remain quiet. That is, let it judge that nothing is either bad or good which can happen equally to the bad man and the good. For what happens equally to the one who lives contrary to nature and to the one who lives according to nature is neither according to nature nor contrary to it.

40\. Constantly regard the universe as a single living being, with one substance and one soul. Observe how all things are referred to the single perception of this one living being; how all things act by one impulse; how all things are joint causes of everything that exists; and observe, too, the continuous spinning of the thread and the close weave of the web.

41\. You are a little soul carrying around a corpse, as Epictetus used to say.

42\. It is no evil for things to undergo change, and no good for things to come into being through change.

43\. Time is a kind of river made up of the things that happen, and a violent torrent. As soon as each thing comes into view it is swept away, and another takes its place, and that too will be swept away.

44\. Everything that happens is as customary and familiar as the rose in spring and fruit in summer. Such are disease, death, slander, treachery, and whatever else delights fools or vexes them.

45\. In the sequence of things, what follows is always fittingly related to what came before. This sequence is not like a mere list of unconnected items whose only order is one of necessity; it is a rational connection. And just as all existing things are harmoniously arranged together, so the things that come into being display no bare succession but a certain wonderful kinship.

46\. Always remember the saying of Heraclitus: that the death of earth is to become water, the death of water is to become air, the death of air is to become fire, and the reverse. Think, too, of the man who forgets where the road leads; and that people quarrel with the very thing they are most constantly in contact with, the reason that governs the universe; and that the things they encounter every day seem strange to them. Consider also that we ought not to act and speak as if we were asleep, since even in sleep we seem to act and speak; and that we ought not, like children taught by their parents, simply to act and speak as we have been taught.

47\. If some god told you that you would die tomorrow, or at any rate the day after, you would not much care whether it was the third day or the next, unless you were utterly mean-spirited, for how small the difference is! In the same way, think it no great matter to die after as many years as you can name rather than tomorrow.

48\. Think continually of how many physicians have died after so often knitting their brows over the sick; how many astrologers after predicting, with great pretension, the deaths of others; how many philosophers after endless discourses on death or immortality; how many heroes after killing thousands; how many tyrants who wielded their power over men's lives with terrible insolence, as though they themselves were immortal; and how many entire cities have, so to speak, died: Helice, swallowed by the sea, and Pompeii and Herculaneum, and countless others. Add to the count all those you have known, one after another. One man buried another and was then laid out dead himself, and yet another buried him, and all within a short time. In short, always observe how fleeting and worthless human things are: what was yesterday a little mucus will tomorrow be a mummy or ashes. Pass, then, through this brief span of time in harmony with nature, and end your journey content, as an olive falls when it is ripe, blessing the nature that produced it and thanking the tree on which it grew.

49\. Be like the headland against which the waves break without ceasing: it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it.

Am I unhappy because this has happened to me? Not so. Rather, I am happy, even though this has happened, because I remain free from pain, neither crushed by the present nor afraid of the future. Something like this might have happened to anyone, but not everyone would have remained free from pain in the face of it. Why, then, should that be counted a misfortune rather than this a piece of good fortune? Do you really, in every case, call something a man's misfortune when it is no departure from human nature? And does anything seem to you a departure from human nature when it does not run against nature's own will? You know what that will is. Will what has happened, then, prevent you from being just, magnanimous, temperate, and prudent; from being secure against rash opinions and falsehood; from having modesty, freedom, and every other quality whose presence allows human nature to possess all that properly belongs to it? Remember, too, whenever something tempts you toward vexation, to apply this principle: not that this is a misfortune, but that to bear it nobly is good fortune.

50\. It is a commonplace aid, but still a useful one, toward despising death to call to mind those who clung tenaciously to life. What more did they gain than those who died early? At last they all lie in their tombs somewhere: Cadicianus, Fabius, Julianus, Lepidus, and anyone else like them, who carried many others out to burial and then were carried out themselves. The whole interval between birth and death is small; and consider with how much trouble it is laboriously passed, in what company, and in how feeble a body. Do not, then, regard life as a thing of any value. Look at the immensity of time behind you, and at the time ahead, another boundless expanse. Within this infinity, what difference is there between one who lives three days and one who, like Homer's Nestor, lives through three generations?

51\. Always take the short road, and the short road is the natural one. Accordingly, say and do everything in keeping with the soundest reason. Such a resolve frees a man from trouble and strife, and from all artifice and ostentation.

## Book V

1\. In the morning, when you rise reluctantly, keep this thought before you: I am rising to do the work of a human being. Why, then, am I discontented if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world? Or was I made for this, to lie under the bedclothes and keep myself warm? "But this is more pleasant." Do you exist, then, for pleasure, and not at all for action and effort? Do you not see the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, and the bees all working together, each setting in order its own part of the universe? And are you unwilling to do the work of a human being, and slow to do what accords with your nature? "But one must also rest." One must. Yet nature has set limits to this as well, just as she has set limits to eating and drinking; and yet you go beyond those limits, beyond what is sufficient, while in your actions you do not, but stop short of what you could do. So you do not love yourself; if you did, you would love your nature and her will. Those who love their particular crafts wear themselves out working at them, unwashed and unfed. But you value your own nature less than the woodturner at his lathe values the art of turning, or the dancer the art of dancing, or the money-lover his money, or the vain man his scrap of glory. When such people are passionately attached to something, they would rather go without food and sleep than fail to perfect the things they care about. Do acts that serve society seem to you meaner than these, and less worthy of your effort?

2\. How easy it is to push away and wipe out every impression that is troublesome or unfitting, and at once to be entirely at peace.

3\. Judge every word and deed that accords with nature to be fitting for you. Do not be turned aside by the criticism that may follow from others, or by their talk; if a thing is good to do or to say, do not consider it beneath you. Those people have their own governing principle and follow their own impulses. Pay no attention to these, but go straight ahead, following your own nature and the common nature; and the path of both is one.

4\. I go on through the things that happen according to nature until I fall and rest, breathing out my breath into that element from which I draw it in each day, and falling upon that earth from which my father gathered the seed, my mother the blood, and my nurse the milk; from which, for so many years, I have been supplied with food and drink; and which bears me when I tread upon it and misuse it for so many purposes.

5\. You say, "People cannot admire the sharpness of my wits." So be it. But there are many other things of which you cannot say, "Nature did not make me for these." Show, then, those qualities that are entirely in your power: sincerity, seriousness, endurance of toil, aversion to pleasure, contentment with your lot and with little, benevolence, frankness, no love of excess, freedom from triviality, magnanimity. Do you not see how many qualities you could display at once, for which there is no excuse of natural incapacity or unfitness, and yet you still willingly fall short of the mark? Or are you compelled, because nature equipped you poorly, to grumble, to be stingy, to flatter, to blame your poor body, to try to please people, to make a great show, and to be so restless in mind? No, by the gods; you could have been rid of these things long ago. If in truth you can be charged only with being somewhat slow and dull of understanding, you must work at this too, neither neglecting it nor taking pleasure in your dullness.

6\. One man, when he has done someone a service, is quick to enter it on his account as a favor bestowed. Another is not so quick to do this, but in his own mind he still regards the other as his debtor, and he knows what he has done. A third, in a sense, does not even know what he has done; he is like a vine that has borne grapes and asks for nothing further once it has produced its proper fruit. As a horse when it has run its race, a dog when it has tracked its quarry, a bee when it has made its honey, so a man when he has done a good deed does not call others to come and look, but passes on to another, as a vine passes on to bear its grapes again in their season. "Must a man, then, be one of those who act this way, almost without noticing?" Yes. "But this very thing is necessary: to observe what one is doing. For it is characteristic, one might say, of a social creature to perceive that he is acting socially, and indeed to wish that his fellow should perceive it too." What you say is true, but you misunderstand what is being said now; and for this reason you will become one of those I mentioned first, for they too are led astray by a certain show of reason. But if you are willing to understand what is meant, do not fear that you will on that account neglect any social act.

7\. A prayer of the Athenians: "Rain, rain, dear Zeus, upon the plowed fields of the Athenians and upon their plains." Truly, we ought either not to pray at all, or to pray in this simple and noble fashion.

8\. When we are told that Aesculapius, the god of healing, prescribed horseback riding for this man, or cold baths, or going barefoot, we understand what is meant; and we must understand it in the same way when we are told that the nature of the universe prescribed for this man disease, or mutilation, or loss, or anything else of the kind. In the first case, "prescribed" means something like this: he ordered it for this man as something suited to restore his health. In the second, it means that whatever befalls each man has been arranged for him in a way that fits his destiny. This is what we mean when we say that things "befit" us, just as builders say that the squared stones in walls or in pyramids fit when they join them to one another in a particular arrangement. There is, in all things, a single fitness, a single harmony. And just as the universe is made up of all bodies to be the body that it is, so destiny is made up of all existing causes to be the cause that it is. Even the wholly uneducated understand what I mean, for they say, "Fate brought this on him." This, then, was brought on him, and this was prescribed for him. Let us accept these things as we accept what Aesculapius prescribes. Many of his prescriptions, naturally, are disagreeable, yet we accept them in the hope of health. Regard the fulfillment and completion of whatever the common nature judges to be good in the same light as your own health. Accept everything that happens, then, even if it seems disagreeable, because it leads to this: to the health of the universe, and to the prosperity and well-being of Zeus, that is, of the universe itself. For he would not have brought upon any man what he has brought, if it were not useful to the whole. Nor does the nature of anything, whatever it may be, bring about anything that is not suited to what it governs. There are, then, two reasons why you should be content with whatever happens to you. The first is that it was done for you and prescribed for you, and in a sense concerned you, spun together with your destiny from the most ancient causes. The second is that even what comes to each individual is, for the power that governs the universe, a cause of its well-being and perfection, and indeed of its very continuance. For the integrity of the whole is mutilated if you cut away anything at all from the coherence and continuity of its parts or of its causes. And you do cut something away, so far as it lies in your power, whenever you are dissatisfied and in a sense try to put something out of the way.

9\. Do not be disgusted, discouraged, or dissatisfied if you do not succeed in doing everything according to right principles. When you have failed, return again, and be content if the greater part of what you do is consistent with human nature, and love that to which you return. Do not go back to philosophy as though she were a schoolmaster, but as people with inflamed eyes go back to their soothing remedy of sponge and egg, or as another turns to a medicinal plaster or a bathing with water. In this way you will not fail to obey reason, and you will find rest in it. Remember that philosophy asks only what your nature asks; but you wanted something else, something not in accordance with nature. "But what," someone may object, "could be more pleasant than what I am doing?" Is this not the very reason pleasure deceives us? Consider whether magnanimity, freedom, simplicity, equanimity, and piety are not more pleasant. For what is more pleasant than wisdom itself, when you consider the security and the smooth course of everything that depends on the faculty of understanding and knowledge?

10\. Things are so wrapped in obscurity that not a few philosophers, and no ordinary ones, have thought them wholly beyond understanding; even the Stoics find them hard to grasp. And all our assent is liable to change, for where is the man who never changes his mind? Turn your thoughts, then, to the objects themselves, and consider how short-lived and worthless they are, and that they may belong to a filthy wretch, a whore, or a robber. Then turn to the characters of those you live among: it is scarcely possible to bear even the most agreeable of them, to say nothing of the fact that a man can scarcely bear himself. In such darkness and filth, in so constant a flux of substance and of time, of motion and of things moved, what there is that deserves to be highly prized, or even seriously pursued, I cannot imagine. On the contrary, a man's duty is to console himself and wait for his natural dissolution, not chafing at the delay, but resting on these two principles alone: first, that nothing will happen to me that is not in keeping with the nature of the universe; and second, that it is in my power never to act against my god and the divine spirit within me, for no one can compel me to do so.

11\. What am I now using my soul for? On every occasion I must put this question to myself and ask: What do I have at this moment in that part of me they call the ruling principle? And whose soul do I have now: a child's, a young man's, a feeble woman's, a tyrant's, a domestic animal's, or a wild beast's?

12\. We can learn what sort of things the many take to be good even from this. If a man has conceived of certain things as truly good, such as prudence, temperance, justice, and courage, then, having once formed that conception, he could not bear to listen to anything out of harmony with what is truly good. But if a man has first conceived as good the things that seem good to the many, he will listen to the line of the comic poet and readily accept it as apt. Thus even the many perceive the difference. Were it not so, the saying would not offend us and be rejected in the first case, while in the second, applied to wealth and to the means of luxury and fame, we accept it as fitting and witty. Go on, then, and ask whether we should prize and think good those things to which, once they have been conceived in the mind, the comic poet's words might aptly be applied: that their owner, out of sheer abundance, has no place left to relieve himself.

13\. I am composed of form and matter, and neither will perish into nonexistence, just as neither came into existence out of nonexistence. Every part of me, then, will be transformed into some part of the universe, and that in turn will change into another part, and so on forever. By a similar process of change I too came into being, and those who begot me, and so on forever in the other direction. Nothing prevents us from speaking this way, even if the universe is governed according to fixed cycles.

14\. Reason, and the art of reasoning that is philosophy, are powers sufficient for themselves and their own works. They start from a first principle that is their own and make their way to the end set before them. That is why such acts are called *katorthoseis*, or right acts, a word that signifies that they proceed along the right road.

15\. None of those things ought to be called a man's that do not belong to a man as a man. They are not demanded of a man, human nature does not promise them, and they are not the means by which human nature reaches its end. Neither the end of man, then, lies in these things, nor what helps to achieve that end, and what helps toward that end is the good. Besides, if any of these things did belong to man, it would not be right for a man to despise them and set himself against them; nor would a man deserve praise for showing he did not need them; nor would one who denied himself any of them be good, if these things were really good. As it is, the more a man strips himself of these things or others like them, or the more patiently he bears being deprived of any of them, the better a man he is in exactly that degree.

16\. Your mind will take on the character of your habitual thoughts, for the soul is dyed by its thoughts. Dye it, then, with a steady succession of thoughts like these: that wherever a man can live, there he can also live well. But he must live in a palace? Very well, then he can also live well in a palace. And again, consider that each thing has been made for whatever purpose it was made, and is carried toward that purpose; that its end lies in what it is carried toward; and that where its end is, there also is its advantage and its good. Now the good of the rational animal is society, for it has already been shown that we are made for society. Is it not plain that the inferior exists for the sake of the superior? And living things are superior to those without life, and among living things, those with reason are superior.

17\. To seek the impossible is madness, and it is impossible that bad men should not do something of this kind.

18\. Nothing happens to any man that he is not formed by nature to bear. The same things happen to another, and either because he does not see that they have happened, or because he wants to display his greatness of spirit, he stands firm and remains unharmed. It is shameful, then, that ignorance and vanity should prove stronger than wisdom.

19\. Things in themselves do not touch the soul in the least. They have no entry into it and cannot turn or move it. The soul alone turns and moves itself, and whatever judgments it sees fit to make, it shapes accordingly the things that present themselves to it.

20\. In one respect man is the thing closest to me, insofar as I must do good to men and bear with them. But insofar as some men set themselves up as obstacles to my proper acts, man becomes to me one of the indifferent things, no less than the sun, the wind, or a wild beast. These may indeed hinder my action, but they are no hindrance to my intentions and disposition, which can act with reservation and adapt themselves. For the mind converts every obstacle to its activity into an aid: what hinders an act becomes a furtherance of it, and what blocks the road helps us along it.

21\. Revere what is highest in the universe: that which makes use of all things and directs all things. And in the same way revere what is highest in yourself, which is of the same kind. For in you, too, it is this that makes use of everything else, and by this your life is directed.

22\. What does no harm to the state does no harm to the citizen. Whenever something appears to harm you, apply this rule: if the state is not harmed by this, neither am I. But if the state is harmed, you must not be angry with the one who harmed it. Show him where he has gone wrong.

23\. Reflect often on how swiftly all things pass by and vanish, both what exists and what is coming into being. For substance is like a river in perpetual flow; the activities of things are in constant change; causes work in endless variety; and scarcely anything stands still. Consider, too, what lies close beside you: this boundless abyss of past and future in which all things disappear. How, then, is he not a fool who is puffed up by such things, or tormented by them until he makes himself miserable? For they trouble him only for a time, and a short time.

24\. Think of universal substance, of which you have a very small share; of universal time, of which a brief and indivisible interval has been assigned to you; and of what is fixed by destiny, and how small a part of it you are.

25\. Does someone wrong me? That is his concern. He has his own disposition and his own activity. I have now what universal nature wills me to have now, and I do what my own nature wills me to do now.

26\. Let the leading and governing part of your soul remain undisturbed by the movements of the flesh, whether of pleasure or of pain. Let it not merge with them, but let it draw a boundary around itself and confine those sensations to their own parts of the body. But when they rise up into the mind through that other sympathy that naturally exists in a body that is all one, you must not try to resist the sensation, for it is natural; only let the ruling part not add to the sensation, of its own accord, the opinion that it is either good or bad.

27\. Live with the gods. And he lives with the gods who constantly shows them that his soul is content with what is allotted to it, and that it does all that is willed by the daemon, the guardian and guide, a portion of himself, that Zeus has given to every man. And this daemon is each man's understanding and reason.

28\. Are you angry with a man whose armpits stink? Angry with one whose breath is foul? What good will your anger do you? He has such a mouth, he has such armpits; such an odor must come from such sources. "But the man has reason," it will be said, "and if he takes the trouble he can discover what is offensive about him." I wish you joy of your discovery. Well, then, you have reason too: use your rational faculty to stir his; show him his error, admonish him. If he listens, you will cure him, and there will be no need for anger. Neither a tragic actor nor a whore.

29\. As you intend to live when you have gone out . . . so it is in your power to live here. But if men will not allow you, then depart from life, yet as though you were suffering no harm. The house is smoky, and I leave it. Why do you think this is any trouble? But so long as nothing of the kind drives me out, I stay, I am free, and no one will prevent me from doing what I choose; and I choose to do what accords with the nature of a rational and social animal.

30\. The intelligence of the universe is social. Accordingly, it has made the inferior things for the sake of the superior, and has fitted the superior to one another. You see how it has subordinated, coordinated, and assigned to each thing its proper share, and has brought the best things into concord with one another.

31\. How have you behaved until now toward the gods, your parents, your brothers and sisters, your children, your teachers, those who cared for you in infancy, your friends, your relatives, your slaves? Consider whether you have so far behaved toward all of them in such a way that this might be said of you:

"Never wronged a man in deed or word."

And call to mind how much you have passed through and how much you have been able to endure; remember that the story of your life is now complete and your service is ended. Recall how many beautiful things you have seen, how many pleasures and pains you have looked down on, how many things called honorable you have spurned, and to how many ill-natured people you have nonetheless shown kindness.

32\. Why should unskilled and ignorant souls disturb one who has skill and knowledge? And which soul has skill and knowledge? The one that knows the beginning and the end, and knows the reason that pervades all substance and governs the universe through all time in fixed, recurring cycles.

33\. Soon, very soon, you will be ashes or a skeleton, and either a name or not even a name; and a name is nothing but sound and echo. The things most prized in life are empty, rotten, and trivial. They are like little dogs biting one another, or little children who quarrel, laugh, and then immediately burst into tears. But fidelity and modesty and justice and truth have fled, as Hesiod says,

Up to Olympus from the wide-spread earth.

What, then, still keeps you here, if the objects of the senses are easily changed and never stand still, if the organs of perception are dull and readily take in false impressions, and if the poor soul itself is merely a vapor rising from the blood? To have a good reputation in a world like this is an empty thing. Why, then, do you not wait calmly for your end, whether it is extinction or a passage into some other state? And until that time comes, what is enough? Nothing other than to revere the gods and bless them, to do good to people, and to practice tolerance and self-restraint, bearing with people and things as they are while keeping your own passions under control. As for everything that lies beyond the limits of this poor flesh and breath, remember that it is neither yours nor in your power.

34\. You can live your life in an even and steady flow of happiness if you can keep to the right road, and think and act in the right way. Two things are common to the soul of God, to the soul of man, and to the soul of every rational being: not to be obstructed by another, and to hold that the good consists in a disposition toward justice and in the practice of it, and to let your desire come to rest there.

35\. If this is neither my own wrongdoing nor a consequence of my wrongdoing, and the common good is not injured, why am I troubled by it? And what harm is done to the common good?

36\. Do not be carried away thoughtlessly by the appearance of things. Help everyone as far as you are able and as far as they are fit to be helped; and if they have suffered a loss in things that are morally indifferent, do not imagine that this is real damage, for to think so is a bad habit. Instead, act as the old man did who, as he was leaving, asked for his foster-child's top, remembering all the while that it was only a top. Do the same in this case.

When you are shouting from the Rostra, the speakers' platform in the Forum, have you forgotten, man, what these things really are? "Yes, but they matter a great deal to these people." Will you, then, let yourself be made a fool over them as well? "I was once a fortunate man, but I lost my good fortune, I do not know how." But to be fortunate means that a man has assigned himself a good fortune, and a good fortune is a good disposition of the soul, good emotions, and good actions.

## Book VI

1\. The substance of the universe is obedient and compliant, and the reason that governs it has within itself no cause for doing evil. It has no malice; it does no evil to anything, and nothing is harmed by it. All things come into being and reach completion according to this reason.

2\. If you are doing your duty, let it make no difference to you whether you are cold or warm, drowsy or well rested, spoken ill of or praised, dying or doing something else. For dying, too, is one of the acts of life, and in this act as in every other, it is enough to do well whatever you have in hand.

3\. Look beneath the surface. Let neither the particular quality of anything nor its true worth escape you.

4\. All existing things soon change. Either they will be turned into vapor, if indeed all substance is one, or they will be scattered.

5\. The governing reason knows its own disposition, what it does, and the material on which it works.

6\. The best way to avenge yourself is not to become like the one who wronged you.

7\. Take pleasure in one thing and rest in it: passing from one act of service to the community to the next, with your thoughts on God.

8\. The ruling principle is that which rouses itself and turns itself. While it makes itself whatever it is and whatever it wills to be, it also makes everything that happens appear to it in whatever way it wills.

9\. Every single thing is accomplished in conformity with the nature of the universe. It is certainly not accomplished according to any other nature, whether one that encloses this nature from outside, or one contained within it, or one wholly external to it and independent of it.

10\. The universe is either a confusion, a mutual tangling of things and a scattering, or else it is unity, order, and providence. If it is the former, why should I wish to linger in a chance assortment of things and such disorder? Why should I care about anything except how I shall at last become earth? And why am I disturbed, since the dispersal of my elements will happen whatever I do? But if the other supposition is true, I revere, I stand firm, and I trust in the one who governs.

11\. When circumstances have forced you into a state of some disturbance, return to yourself quickly, and do not stay out of tune any longer than the pressure lasts. You will gain greater mastery over the harmony by constantly coming back to it.

12\. If you had a stepmother and a mother at the same time, you would be dutiful toward your stepmother, but you would still keep returning to your mother. Let the imperial court and philosophy be stepmother and mother to you now. Return to philosophy often and take your rest in her, for it is through her that what you meet with at court seems bearable to you, and you seem bearable to the court.

13\. When we have meat and other such dishes in front of us, we form the impression that this is the dead body of a fish, and this the dead body of a bird or a pig; and again, that this Falernian, the most celebrated of Italian wines, is only a little grape juice, and this purple robe is some sheep's wool dyed with the blood of a shellfish. Impressions of this kind reach the things themselves and pierce through them, and so we see what sort of things they really are. We ought to do the same throughout our lives. Where things seem most deserving of our approval, we ought to lay them bare, look at their worthlessness, and strip away all the words by which they are exalted. For outward show is a remarkable perverter of reason, and just when you are most certain that you are occupied with things worth your trouble, that is when it deceives you most. Consider, then, what Crates says about Xenocrates himself.

14\. Most of the things the multitude admire belong to the most general class of objects: those held together merely by cohesion or by natural growth, such as stones, wood, fig trees, vines, and olives. Those admired by people who are somewhat more reasonable belong to the class of things held together by a living principle, such as flocks and herds. Those admired by people who are still better educated are things held together by a rational soul. Yet this is not a universal soul, but one that is rational only insofar as it is skilled in some art or expert in some other way, or simply insofar as it owns a great number of slaves. But the person who values a rational soul, a soul that is universal and fitted for life in a political community, regards nothing else but this. Above all, he keeps his own soul in a condition and an activity in keeping with reason and social life, and he works together toward this end with those who are of the same kind as himself.

15\. Some things are hurrying into existence, and others are hurrying out of it; and of what is coming into existence, part is already extinguished. Motion and change are constantly renewing the world, just as the unbroken course of time is always renewing the endless succession of ages. In this flowing stream, then, where nothing stays put, which of the things rushing past would anyone set a high value on? It would be like falling in love with one of the sparrows flying by, when it has already passed out of sight. The very life of every person is something of this kind, like the vapor rising from the blood and the drawing in of air. For just as we take in a breath and give it back again, as we do every moment, so it is with the whole power of breathing, which you received at your birth only yesterday or the day before, and which you must give back to the element from which you first drew it.

16\. Neither transpiration, as in plants, is worth valuing, nor breathing, as in domesticated animals and wild beasts, nor receiving impressions from the appearances of things, nor being jerked about by desires like a puppet on strings, nor gathering in herds, nor being nourished by food, for feeding is on a level with separating out and discarding the useless part of what we eat. What, then, is worth valuing? Being received with clapping hands? No. Nor should we value the clapping of tongues, for the praise of the many is nothing more than that. Suppose, then, that you have given up this worthless thing called fame. What remains that is worth valuing? In my opinion, this: to move yourself and to hold yourself back in keeping with your proper constitution, which is the end toward which all occupations and arts lead. Every art aims at making the thing it produces fit for the work it was made to do. The vine grower who tends the vine, the horse breaker, and the dog trainer all pursue this end. The education and teaching of the young aim at something as well, and their value lies in this. If this is in good order, you will not look for anything else. Will you not stop valuing many other things as well? If you do not, you will be neither free, nor sufficient for your own happiness, nor free from passion. You will inevitably be envious, jealous, and suspicious of those who can take those things away, and you will plot against those who possess what you value. Anyone who wants any of these things is bound to live in a state of constant agitation, and on top of that he will often find fault with the gods. But to revere and honor your own mind will make you content with yourself, in harmony with society, and in agreement with the gods, which means praising everything they give and have ordained.

17\. Above, below, and all around are the movements of the elements. But the motion of virtue belongs to none of these. It is something more divine, and advancing along a path that is scarcely noticed, it goes happily on its way.

18\. How strangely people behave! They will not praise those who live in their own time and in their own company, yet they set great store by being praised by posterity, by people they have never seen and never will see. But this is much the same as if you were to be upset because those who lived before you did not praise you.

19\. If something is hard for you to accomplish, do not assume that it is impossible for human beings. But if something is possible for a human being and in keeping with human nature, assume that you can achieve it too.

20\. Suppose that in the exercises of the gymnasium someone has scratched you with his nails or given you a gash by butting you in the head. We show no sign of irritation, we take no offense, and we do not suspect him afterward of being treacherous. We are on our guard against him, yet not as an enemy, nor even with suspicion; we simply and quietly keep out of his way. Let your conduct be something like this in every other part of life. Let us overlook many things in those who are, as it were, our sparring partners in the gymnasium. For it is in our power, as I said, to get out of the way, and to feel neither suspicion nor hatred.

21\. If anyone can convince me and show me that I am not thinking or acting rightly, I will gladly change. For I am seeking the truth, and no one was ever harmed by the truth. The one who is harmed is the person who persists in his own error and ignorance.

22\. I do my duty, and other things do not trouble me, for they are either lifeless, or without reason, or have wandered off and do not know the way.

23\. As for the animals that lack reason, and in general all things and objects, use them with a generous and liberal spirit, since you have reason and they have none. But toward human beings, since they have reason, behave in a spirit of fellowship. On every occasion call upon the gods, and do not trouble yourself about how long you will be able to do this, for even three hours spent in this way are enough.

24\. Alexander of Macedon and his groom were brought to the same condition by death. Either they were both taken back into the same generative principles of the universe, or they were both alike scattered among the atoms.

25\. Consider how many things happen within each of us in the same indivisible moment, things concerning the body and things concerning the soul. Then you will not be surprised if many more things, or rather all the things that come into being in that which is the one and the whole, which we call the Cosmos, exist in it at the same time.

26\. If someone asked you how the name Antoninus is spelled, would you shout out each letter at the top of your voice? And if they grew angry, would you grow angry too? Would you not instead go on calmly and count out every letter? So too in this life, remember that every duty is made up of certain parts. It is your duty to attend to these and, without being disturbed or showing anger toward those who are angry with you, to go on your way and finish the task set before you.

27\. How cruel it is to forbid people to pursue what appears to them suitable to their nature and to their advantage! And yet, in a sense, you do forbid them this when you are vexed at their wrongdoing. For they are certainly drawn toward things because they suppose those things to be suitable to their nature and to their advantage. "But they are not." Then teach them and show them, without being angry.

28\. Death is a cessation of the impressions that come through the senses, of the pulling of the strings that move the appetites, of the restless wandering of thought, and of service to the flesh.

29\. It is a disgrace for the soul to be the first to give out in this life while the body still holds up.

30\. Take care that you are not turned into a Caesar, that you are not steeped in that imperial purple, for such things do happen. Keep yourself, then, simple, good, pure, serious, unaffected, a friend of justice, a worshipper of the gods, kind, affectionate, and vigorous in every proper action. Strive to remain the person philosophy wanted to make of you. Revere the gods, and help human beings. Life is short. There is only one fruit of this earthly life: a reverent disposition and acts done for the common good. Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus. Remember his constancy in every act that accorded with reason, his evenness in all things, his piety, the serenity of his face, his gentleness, his indifference to empty fame, and his efforts to understand things. Remember how he would never let anything pass without first examining it with the greatest care and understanding it clearly; how he put up with those who blamed him unjustly without blaming them in return; how he did nothing in a hurry; how he would not listen to slander; and how exact an examiner of character and conduct he was. He was not given to reproaching people, nor was he timid, suspicious, or a sophist. Remember how little he needed in the way of lodging, bed, clothing, food, and servants; how hard he worked and how patient he was; and how, thanks to his sparing diet, he could hold out until evening, not even needing to relieve himself except at the usual hour. Remember his steadfastness and consistency in his friendships, the way he tolerated frank speech from those who opposed his opinions, the pleasure he took when anyone showed him something better, and how religious he was without being superstitious. Imitate all this, so that when your last hour comes you may have as clear a conscience as he had.

31\. Come back to your senses and call yourself back. And when you have roused yourself from sleep and seen that it was only dreams that troubled you, then, now that you are awake, look at the things around you as you looked at those dreams.

32\. I consist of a little body and a soul. To this little body all things are indifferent, for it is incapable of perceiving differences. But to the understanding, only those things are indifferent that are not the products of its own activity. Whatever things are the products of its own activity are all within its power. And even among these, only those that concern the present matter, for the mind's future and past activities are themselves, for the present, indifferent.

33\. Neither the work of the hand nor that of the foot is contrary to nature, so long as the foot does the foot's work and the hand the hand's. Likewise, a person's labor is not contrary to nature, so long as it does the work of a human being. And if the labor is not contrary to his nature, neither is it an evil to him.

34\. How many pleasures have been enjoyed by robbers, parricides, and tyrants.

35\. Do you not see how craftsmen accommodate themselves, up to a point, to those who have no skill in their craft, yet cling to the principles of their art and refuse to depart from them? Is it not strange that the architect and the physician should have more respect for the principles of their own arts than a human being has for his own reason, which he shares with the gods?

36\. Asia and Europe are corners of the universe; the whole sea is a drop in the universe; Mount Athos is a little clod of the universe; all of present time is a point in eternity. All things are small, changeable, and perishable. All things come from that one source, from the universal ruling power, either proceeding from it directly or following from it as consequences. And so the gaping jaws of the lion, poison, and every harmful thing, such as a thorn or mud, are by-products of what is grand and beautiful. Do not imagine, then, that they are of a different kind from what you revere, but form a just opinion of the source of all things.

37\. Whoever has seen what exists now has seen everything, both all that has happened from eternity and all that will be for time without end, for all things are of one kin and one form.

38\. Reflect often on the connection of all things in the universe and their relation to one another. In a sense, all things are woven together, and in this way all are in friendly accord with one another. For one thing follows another in order, and this comes about through the active movement that runs through them, the common breath they share, and the unity of their substance.

39\. Adapt yourself to the circumstances in which your lot has been cast, and love the people among whom you have received your portion. But love them truly, and sincerely.

40\. Every instrument, tool, or vessel is in good order if it does what it was made to do, even though its maker is no longer present. But in things held together by nature, the power that made them is within them and remains there. All the more reason, then, to revere this power, and to recognize that if you live and act according to its will, everything in you is in keeping with intelligence. In the same way, the things that belong to the universe are in keeping with intelligence.

41\. Whatever thing outside your control you suppose to be good or evil for you, it must follow that, if such an evil befalls you or such a good is lost, you will blame the gods and hate the people who are responsible for the misfortune or the loss, or those you suspect of being likely to be responsible. Indeed, we commit a great deal of injustice because we do not regard these things as indifferent. But if we judge only those things that are within our power to be good or bad, there is no reason left either to find fault with God or to take a hostile stance toward other people.

42\. We are all working together toward one end, some knowingly and with purpose, others without knowing what they do. In the same way, I think it is Heraclitus who says that even people who are asleep are laborers and fellow workers in what takes place in the universe. But people cooperate in different ways. Even those who complain about what happens, and those who try to resist it and obstruct it, cooperate in full measure, because the universe has need even of such people as these. It remains for you, then, to understand what kind of workers you are placing yourself among. He who governs all things will certainly make right use of you, and will take you in as one of the fellow workers whose labors contribute to a single end. But do not be that kind of part which Chrysippus spoke of: the cheap and ridiculous line in a comedy, worthless in itself, which nonetheless plays its part in the whole play.

43\. Does the sun take on the work of the rain, or Aesculapius, the god of healing, the work of the Fruit-Bearer, the earth? And what of each of the stars? Are they not different from one another, and yet they work together toward the same end?

44\. If the gods have made decisions about me and about the things that must happen to me, they have decided well, for it is not easy even to imagine a god without foresight. And why would they have any desire to do me harm? What advantage would come of it, either to them or to the whole, which is the particular object of their providence? But if they have made no decisions about me individually, they have certainly made decisions about the whole, and the things that happen as consequences of that general order I ought to accept with pleasure and be content with. But suppose they decide about nothing at all. To believe this is wicked, and if we do believe it, then let us neither sacrifice, nor pray, nor swear by them, nor do any of the other things we do on the assumption that the gods are present and live among us. Still, if the gods decide nothing about anything that concerns us, I am able to decide about myself, and I can inquire into what is useful. And what is useful to each person is what accords with his own constitution and nature. My nature is rational and social. My city and my country, so far as I am Antoninus, is Rome; but so far as I am a human being, it is the world. The things that are useful to these cities, then, are the only things useful to me.

45\. Whatever happens to each person happens in the interest of the universe. That alone might be enough. But if you pay attention, you will also notice this as a general truth: whatever is profitable to one person is profitable to others as well. Here, however, let the word "profitable" be taken in its ordinary sense, as it applies to things of the middle kind, which are neither good nor bad.

46\. In the amphitheater and similar places, the constant sight of the same things, and their sameness, makes the spectacle tiresome. So it is with the whole of life, for everything above and below is the same and comes from the same sources. How long, then, must this go on?

47\. Think constantly of how people of every kind, of every occupation, and of every nation are dead, and let your thoughts descend even to such figures as Philistion, Phoebus, and Origanion. Then turn your thoughts to the other kinds of people. We must go to the place where there are so many great orators and so many noble philosophers, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Socrates; so many heroes of former days, and so many generals after them, and tyrants; and besides these, Eudoxus, Hipparchus, Archimedes, and other men of sharp natural gifts, great minds, lovers of work, versatile, self-assured, and even mockers of the perishable and fleeting life of man, like Menippus and those like him. Consider that all of these have long been in the dust. What harm is this to them? And what harm to those whose very names are unknown? Only one thing here is worth much: to live your life in truth and justice, with goodwill even toward liars and the unjust.

48\. When you want to cheer yourself, think of the virtues of the people around you: the energy of one, the modesty of another, the generosity of a third, and some other good quality of a fourth. Nothing gives such delight as examples of the virtues when they appear in the characters of those we live among, and present themselves in abundance, as far as possible. That is why we must keep them before us.

49\. You are not dissatisfied, I suppose, because you weigh only so many litrae, the pound-weight of the day, and not three hundred. Do not be dissatisfied, then, that you must live only so many years and no more. Just as you are content with the amount of substance assigned to you, be content with the amount of time.

50\. Try to persuade people. But act even against their will when the principles of justice lead that way. If, however, someone uses force to stand in your way, turn to contentment and calm, and at the same time use the obstacle as an occasion to exercise some other virtue. Remember that your effort was made with a reservation, on a condition, and that you did not wish to do the impossible. What, then, did you wish for? An effort of just this kind. And you have achieved your aim, even if the things you set out to do are not accomplished.

51\. The person who loves fame considers another person's activity to be his own good; the one who loves pleasure, his own sensations; but the one who has understanding considers his own actions to be his own good.

52\. It is in our power to form no opinion about a thing and to keep our soul undisturbed, for things themselves have no natural power to shape our judgments.

53\. Train yourself to listen carefully to what another person says, and, as far as possible, to enter into the mind of the speaker.

54\. What is not good for the swarm is not good for the bee.

55\. If sailors abused the helmsman, or the sick abused their doctor, would they listen to anyone else? And how could the helmsman secure the safety of those on board, or the doctor the health of those in his care?

56\. How many of those who came into the world with me have already left it.

57\. To people with jaundice honey tastes bitter; to those bitten by mad dogs water brings terror; and to small children a ball is a fine thing. Why, then, am I angry? Do you think a false opinion has less power than bile in the jaundiced, or poison in someone bitten by a mad dog?

58\. No one will prevent you from living according to the reason of your own nature, and nothing will happen to you contrary to the reason of universal nature.

59\. What kind of people are these whom others wish to please, for what ends, and by what kinds of actions? How soon time will cover all things, and how much it has covered already.

## Book VII

1\. What is wickedness? It is what you have often seen. And whenever anything happens, keep this in mind: it is what you have often seen. Everywhere, high and low, you will find the same things, the things that fill the old histories, those of the middle ages and those of our own day, the things that fill cities and households now. There is nothing new. All things are both familiar and short-lived.

2\. How can our principles die, unless the impressions that correspond to them are extinguished? But it is in your power to keep fanning those thoughts continually into a flame. I can hold whatever opinion I ought to hold about anything. If I can, why am I disturbed? Things outside my mind have no bearing at all on my mind. Let your feelings be ordered this way, and you stand upright. It is in your power to recover your life. Look at things again as you used to look at them, for that is what recovering your life consists in.

3\. The empty business of display, plays on the stage, flocks of sheep, herds of cattle, drills with spears, a bone tossed to little dogs, a crumb of bread thrown into fishponds, the toiling of ants and their burden-carrying, the scurrying of frightened little mice, puppets jerked on strings: all alike. Your duty, then, in the midst of such things, is to show good humor and not a disdainful air, while understanding that every person is worth exactly as much as the things he busies himself with.

4\. In conversation you must pay attention to what is said, and in every movement you must observe what is being done. In the one case you should see at once what end it aims at; in the other, watch carefully for what is meant.

5\. Is my understanding equal to this task or not? If it is, I use it for the work as a tool given to me by universal nature. If it is not, then either I step aside from the work and give way to someone who can do it better, unless there is some reason why I ought not to; or I do it as well as I can, taking as my helper someone who, with the assistance of my own guiding reason, can accomplish what is now fitting and useful for the common good. For whatever I can do, whether alone or with another, ought to be directed toward this alone: what is useful and well suited to society.

6\. How many who were once celebrated by fame have been handed over to oblivion; and how many who celebrated the fame of others have long been dead.

7\. Do not be ashamed to accept help. Your business is to do your duty like a soldier storming a town. What, then, if you are lame and cannot climb the battlements alone, but can do it with someone else's help?

8\. Do not let the future disturb you. You will come to it, if you must, carrying the same reason you now use for the present.

9\. All things are interwoven with one another, and the bond is sacred; there is hardly anything unconnected with something else. For things have been arranged together, and they combine to form the same universe, the same order. There is one universe made up of all things, one god who pervades all things, one substance, one law, one common reason in all intelligent creatures, and one truth, if indeed there is also one perfection for all creatures of the same stock who share in reason.

10\. Everything material quickly vanishes into the substance of the whole; everything formal, or causal, is very soon taken back into universal reason; and the memory of everything is very soon buried by time.

11\. For a rational creature, the same act is in accordance with nature and in accordance with reason.

12\. Stand upright, or be set upright.

13\. Just as the limbs are related within bodies that form a single unity, so are rational beings, though they exist separately, for they have been made for a single shared work. You will perceive this more clearly if you often say to yourself, "I am a member, a limb, of the system of rational beings." The Greek word for this is *melos*. But if you change a single letter to *r* and say that you are merely a part, *meros*, then you do not yet love people from the heart. Doing good does not yet delight you for its own sake. You still do it merely as a matter of propriety, and not yet as doing good to yourself.

14\. Let whatever will fall from outside upon those parts of me that can feel its effects. The parts that have felt it may complain, if they choose. But I am not injured unless I judge that what has happened is an evil, and it is in my power not to judge so.

15\. Whatever anyone does or says, I must be good, just as if gold, or emerald, or purple were always saying this: "Whatever anyone does or says, I must be emerald and keep my color."

16\. The ruling faculty does not disturb itself. I mean that it does not frighten itself or cause itself pain. If someone else can frighten or pain it, let him do so. For the faculty itself will not, by its own judgment, turn in such directions. Let the body take care, if it can, that it suffers nothing, and let it speak up if it does suffer. But the soul itself, the part that is subject to fear and pain and has complete power to form opinions about them, will suffer nothing, for it will never turn aside into such a judgment. The ruling principle, in itself, lacks nothing unless it creates a need for itself. It is therefore free from disturbance and unhindered, provided it does not disturb and hinder itself.

17\. *Eudaemonia*, the Greek word for happiness, means literally a good *daemon*, a good guardian spirit, or a good thing. What, then, are you doing here, imagination? Go away, I beg you by the gods, the way you came, for I do not want you. But you have come in your old fashion. I am not angry with you. Only go away.

18\. Is anyone afraid of change? Why, what can come about without change? What, then, is more pleasing or more fitting to universal nature? Can you take a bath unless the firewood undergoes a change? Can you be nourished unless the food undergoes a change? Can anything else useful be accomplished without change? Do you not see, then, that your own change is exactly the same, and just as necessary to universal nature?

19\. All bodies are carried through the universal substance as through a raging torrent, being by nature united and working together with the whole, as the parts of our body do with one another. How many a Chrysippus, how many a Socrates, how many an Epictetus has time already swallowed up! Let the same thought come to you about every person and every thing.

20\. One thing alone troubles me: that I might do something the constitution of man does not allow, or in a way it does not allow, or at a time it does not allow.

21\. Near at hand is your forgetting of all things, and near at hand is all things' forgetting of you.

22\. It is a distinctly human trait to love even those who do wrong. This happens if, when they do wrong, it occurs to you that they are your kin, that they do wrong through ignorance and without intending it, that soon both of you will be dead, and above all that the wrongdoer has done you no harm, for he has not made your ruling faculty any worse than it was before.

23\. Universal nature, working with universal substance as though it were wax, now molds a horse, and when it has broken that up, it uses the material for a tree, then for a man, then for something else, and each of these things lasts only a very short time. But it is no hardship for the vessel to be broken apart, just as there was none in its being put together.

24\. A scowling face is wholly contrary to nature. When it is put on often, the result is that all beauty dies away, until at last it is so thoroughly extinguished that it can never be rekindled. Try to conclude from this very fact that scowling is contrary to reason. For if even the awareness of doing wrong is lost, what reason is left for living any longer?

25\. The nature that governs the whole will soon change everything you see, and out of its substance will make other things, and other things again out of theirs, so that the world may always be new.

26\. When someone has wronged you, consider at once what opinion about good or evil led him to do it. Once you see this, you will pity him, and you will be neither surprised nor angry. For either you yourself consider the same thing to be good that he does, or something of the same kind, in which case it is your duty to forgive him. Or, if you do not consider such things to be good or evil, you will more readily be well disposed toward someone who is in error.

27\. Think less of what you lack than of what you have. From the things you have, pick out the best, and then reflect how eagerly you would have sought them if you did not have them. At the same time, take care that in being so pleased with them you do not grow used to overvaluing them, so that you would be upset if you ever lost them.

28\. Withdraw into yourself. The ruling rational principle has this nature: it is content with itself when it acts justly, and in this way it secures tranquillity.

29\. Wipe out imagination. Stop the jerking of the puppet strings. Confine yourself to the present. Understand clearly what is happening, whether to you or to another. Divide and analyze every object into its causal, or formal, element and its material element. Think of your last hour. Let the wrong someone has done stay where the wrong was done.

30\. Give your full attention to what is said. Let your understanding enter into the things being done and into the agents who do them.

31\. Adorn yourself with simplicity and modesty, and with indifference toward the things that lie between virtue and vice. Love humankind. Follow God. The poet says that law rules all things. And it is enough to remember that law rules all things.

32\. On death: whether it is a scattering, or a dissolution into atoms, or annihilation, it is either extinction or change.

33\. On pain: pain that is unbearable carries us off; pain that lasts a long time is bearable. The mind preserves its own calm by withdrawing into itself, and the ruling faculty is not made worse. As for the parts that are harmed by the pain, let them, if they can, give their own opinion of it.

34\. On fame: look at the minds of those who seek it, see what they are like, what sort of things they avoid, and what sort of things they pursue. And consider that just as heaps of sand, piled one upon another, bury the sand that was there before, so in life the events that come first are soon covered by those that follow.

35\. From Plato: "The man who has an elevated mind and surveys all time and all existence, do you suppose he can think that human life is anything great?" "It is not possible," he said. "Then such a man will not think death an evil either?" "Certainly not."

36\. From Antisthenes: It is royal to do good and to be spoken ill of.

37\. It is shameful for the face to obey the mind, arranging and composing itself as the mind commands, while the mind is not arranged and composed by itself.

38\. It is not right to vex ourselves at things, for they care nothing about it.

39\. Give joy to the immortal gods and to us.

40\. Life must be reaped like ripened ears of grain: one man is born, another dies.

41\. If the gods care nothing for me and my children, there is a reason for it.

42\. For the good is on my side, and the just.

43\. No joining others in their wailing, no violent emotion.

44\. From Plato: "But I would give this man a fitting answer, namely this: You are mistaken if you think that a man who is good for anything at all ought to calculate the risk of living or dying, rather than look to this alone in everything he does: whether he is acting justly or unjustly, and doing the work of a good man or of a bad one."

45\. "For this is the truth of it, men of Athens: wherever a man has stationed himself, believing it the best place for him, or has been stationed by his commander, there, in my opinion, he ought to stay and face the danger, taking nothing into account, neither death nor anything else, before the disgrace of deserting his post."

46\. "But, my good friend, consider whether what is noble and good may not be something other than saving and being saved. As for living this or that length of time, a man who is truly a man should consider whether this is not something to dismiss from his thoughts. There must be no clinging to life. He must leave these matters to the god and believe what the women say, that no one can escape his destiny, and then turn to the next question: how he may best live the time he has to live."

47\. Watch the courses of the stars as though you were traveling along with them, and keep considering how the elements change into one another, for such thoughts wash away the grime of earthly life.

48\. This is a fine saying of Plato: that whoever is speaking about human beings should look upon earthly things as though from some height above, and see them in their assemblies, armies, farming labors, marriages, treaties, births, deaths, the uproar of the law courts, desert places, the various barbarian nations, festivals, mourning, markets: a mixture of everything, and an orderly combination of opposites.

49\. Consider the past, the great changes in political supremacy, and you can foresee what is to come as well. For it will certainly take the same form, and cannot depart from the pattern of what happens now. To have observed human life for forty years, therefore, is the same as to have observed it for ten thousand. What more will you see?

50\. What has grown from the earth returns to the earth,
But what has sprung from heavenly seed
Goes back again to the heavenly realms.

That is, it is either a loosening of the interlocked atoms, or some similar scattering of the elements that have no feeling.

51\. With food and drink and cunning magic arts
They try to turn the channel's course and so escape from death.

The wind that heaven has sent
We must endure, and labor without complaint.

52\. Another man may be more skilled at throwing his opponent in wrestling; but he is not more social, nor more modest, nor better trained to meet whatever happens, nor more considerate toward the faults of his neighbors.

53\. Wherever any work can be done in keeping with the reason that is common to gods and men, there we have nothing to fear. For where we can gain benefit through activity that succeeds and proceeds according to our constitution, no harm need be suspected.

54\. Everywhere and at all times, it is within your power to accept your present circumstances with reverence, to deal justly with the people around you, and to bring all your skill to bear on your present thoughts, so that nothing slips into them unexamined.

55\. Do not look around to discover the ruling principles of other people. Look straight at where nature is leading you: universal nature through the things that happen to you, and your own nature through the acts you must perform. Every being ought to do what accords with its constitution. All other things have been constituted for the sake of rational beings, just as among irrational things the lower exist for the sake of the higher; but rational beings exist for the sake of one another.

The first principle of the human constitution, then, is the social. The second is not to yield to the persuasions of the body. It is the distinctive function of rational, intelligent motion to set its own boundaries and never to be overpowered by the motions of the senses or of the appetites, since both belong to our animal nature. The intelligent motion claims supremacy and will not allow itself to be mastered by the others, and rightly so, for nature formed it to make use of them all. The third element in the rational constitution is freedom from error and deception. Let the ruling principle hold fast to these things and go straight ahead, and it has what belongs to it.

56\. Consider yourself already dead, your life complete up to this moment, and live whatever remains to you in accordance with nature.

57\. Love only what happens to you, what is spun into the thread of your destiny. What could be more fitting?

58\. In everything that happens, keep before your eyes the people to whom the same things happened: how they were distressed, how they treated these things as strange, how they complained about them. And where are they now? Nowhere. Why, then, would you choose to behave the same way? Why not leave these agitations, which are foreign to nature, to those who stir them up and those who are stirred by them, and give your whole attention to making the right use of whatever happens to you? Then you will use it well, and it will become material for you to work on. Only attend to yourself, and resolve to be a good man in every act you perform; and remember . . .

59\. Look within. Within is the fountain of good, and it will keep bubbling up if you keep digging.

60\. The body should be composed and steady, showing no disorder either in movement or in posture. The mind keeps the face thoughtful and seemly, and the same intelligence and decorum should be expected of the whole body. But all of this should be observed without affectation.

61\. The art of living is more like wrestling than dancing, in that it must stand ready and firm to meet sudden and unexpected attacks.

62\. Keep observing whose approval you want and what ruling principles they hold. Once you look into the sources of their opinions and appetites, you will neither blame those who offend involuntarily nor want their approval.

63\. Every soul, the philosopher says, is deprived of truth against its will; and in the same way it is deprived of justice, temperance, benevolence, and every such quality. It is essential to keep this constantly in mind, for it will make you gentler toward everyone.

64\. In every pain, keep this thought ready: there is no disgrace in it, and it does not make your governing intelligence any worse, for it does not damage that intelligence either insofar as it is rational or insofar as it is social. In most pains, let this saying of Epicurus help you: pain is neither unbearable nor endless, so long as you remember that it has limits and add nothing to it in imagination. Remember, too, that many disagreeable things are pain in disguise, though we fail to recognize them as such: excessive drowsiness, for instance, or being scorched by heat, or having no appetite. So when you find yourself discontented with any of these, tell yourself that you are giving in to pain.

65\. Take care not to feel toward the inhuman as they feel toward human beings.

66\. How do we know that Telauges was not superior in character to Socrates? It is not enough that Socrates died a nobler death, argued more skillfully with the Sophists, and endured a night in the cold with greater fortitude. Nor is it enough that, when ordered to arrest Leon of Salamis, he judged it nobler to refuse, or that he swaggered through the streets, as Aristophanes portrayed him doing in the *Clouds*, though one may well doubt whether that was true. What we should ask is what kind of soul Socrates possessed. Could he be content to be just toward men and devout toward the gods, neither vexed to no purpose by human wickedness nor making himself a slave to anyone's ignorance? Did he refuse to treat anything allotted to him by the universe as strange, or to endure it as unbearable? And did he keep his understanding from sharing in the passions of the wretched flesh?

67\. Nature has not blended intelligence so thoroughly into the composition of the body that you lack the power to set your own boundaries and to bring under your control everything that is yours. It is entirely possible to be a godlike man and be recognized as such by no one. Always keep this in mind, and this as well: very little indeed is needed to live a happy life. And just because you have given up hope of becoming a skilled logician or an expert in the study of nature, do not on that account abandon the hope of being free and modest, sociable and obedient to God.

68\. It is in your power to live free from all compulsion, in the deepest tranquility of mind, even if the whole world cries out against you as loudly as it likes, and even if wild beasts tear apart the limbs of this kneaded lump of matter that has grown around you. For what prevents the mind, amid all of this, from keeping its tranquility, from forming a just judgment of everything around it, and from making ready use of whatever is presented to it? Judgment can then say to whatever comes under its observation, "This is what you are in reality, though in common opinion you may seem to be something else." And use can say to whatever comes to hand, "You are the very thing I was looking for." For to me, whatever presents itself is always material for virtue, both rational and civic, and in short for the exercise of the art that belongs to man or to God. Everything that happens bears some relation either to God or to man. Nothing that happens is new or hard to handle; all of it is familiar and fit material to work on.

69\. Moral perfection consists in living each day as if it were your last, without frenzy, without torpor, and without pretense.

70\. The gods, who are immortal, are not annoyed at having to put up, through so vast a stretch of time, with human beings as they are, so many of them wicked; on top of this, they care for them in every way. But you, who are destined to end so soon: are you tired of putting up with the wicked, and this when you are one of them yourself?

71\. It is absurd for a man not to flee his own wickedness, which is possible, and instead to try to flee the wickedness of others, which is impossible.

72\. Whatever the rational and social faculty finds to be neither intelligent nor social, it rightly judges to be beneath it.

73\. When you have done a good deed and another person has benefited from it, why do you look for a third thing as well, as fools do, whether a reputation for having done good or some return?

74\. No one tires of receiving what is useful. But acting according to nature is useful. Do not, then, tire of receiving what is useful by doing it for others.

75\. The nature of the whole was moved to create the universe. Now, either everything that happens comes about as a consequence of that act, in continuous sequence from it, or else even the most important things, toward which the ruling power of the universe directs its own movement, are governed by no rational principle. If you remember this, it will make you calmer about many things.

## Book VIII

1\. This reflection, too, helps to rid you of the desire for empty fame: it is no longer in your power to have lived your whole life, or at least your life since youth, as a philosopher. On the contrary, it is plain to many others and to yourself that you are far from philosophy. You have fallen into disorder, then, so that it is no longer easy for you to win a philosopher's reputation, and your station in life works against it as well. If you have truly seen where the matter stands, throw away the question of how you will appear to others, and be content if you can live the rest of your life as your nature wills. Observe, then, what it wills, and let nothing else distract you. You have wandered a great deal without finding happiness anywhere: not in syllogisms, not in wealth, not in reputation, not in pleasure, not anywhere. Where, then, is it? In doing what human nature requires. And how is a man to do this? By holding principles from which his feelings and his actions flow. Which principles? Those concerning good and bad: the conviction that nothing is good for a human being that does not make him just, temperate, courageous, and free, and that nothing is bad that does not make him the opposite.

2\. Before every act, ask yourself: How does this bear on me? Will I regret it? A little while and I am dead, and everything is gone. What more do I want, if what I am doing now is the work of an intelligent living being, a social being, one who lives under the same law as God?

3\. Alexander, Gaius Julius Caesar, and Pompey the Great: what are they compared with Diogenes, Heraclitus, and Socrates? These philosophers understood things, along with their causes and their matter, and their ruling principles were in harmony with their pursuits. But as for the others, think how many things they had to worry about, and how many things they were enslaved to!

4\. Remember that people will go on doing the same things regardless, even if you burst.

5\. This is the main thing: do not be disturbed, for everything happens according to the nature of the whole, and in a little while you will be no one and nowhere, like Hadrian and Augustus. Next, fix your eyes steadily on the task in front of you and look at it closely. Remember that it is your duty to be a good man, and remember what human nature demands. Then do that without turning aside, and speak as seems to you most just, only let it be with goodwill, with modesty, and without hypocrisy.

6\. The nature of the whole has this work to do: to move what is here to there, to transform things, to take them from one place and carry them to another. All things are change, yet we need not fear anything new. Everything is familiar, and even the way things are distributed remains the same.

7\. Every nature is content with itself when it proceeds well on its way. A rational nature proceeds well when its thoughts assent to nothing false or uncertain, when it directs its impulses toward social acts alone, when it limits its desires and aversions to the things within its power, and when it is satisfied with everything the common nature assigns to it. For every particular nature is a part of this common nature, as the nature of a leaf is part of the nature of the plant. There is one difference. In the plant, the nature of the leaf belongs to a nature that lacks perception and reason and can be obstructed. Human nature, by contrast, belongs to a nature that cannot be obstructed and is intelligent and just, since it gives each thing its share of time, substance, form, activity, and circumstance, in equal portions and according to its worth. But in examining this, do not expect to find that any single thing, compared with any other single thing, is equal in every respect. Compare instead all the parts of one thing, taken together, with all the parts of another, taken together.

8\. You have no leisure, or no ability, to read. But you do have the leisure to check arrogance; you have the leisure to rise above pleasure and pain; you have the leisure to rise above the love of fame, and not to be irritated by stupid and ungrateful people, and even to care for them.

9\. Let no one hear you complaining any longer about life at court, or about your own life.

10\. Repentance is a kind of self-reproach for having neglected something useful. Now, what is good must be useful, and the truly good man should attend to it. But no such man would ever regret having refused a sensual pleasure. Pleasure, then, is neither good nor useful.

11\. This thing: what is it in itself, in its own constitution? What is its substance and material? What is its causal nature, or form? What is it doing in the world? And how long will it last?

12\. When you get up from sleep reluctantly, remember that performing social acts is in keeping with your constitution and with human nature, whereas sleep is something you share with irrational animals. And what accords with each creature's own nature is more truly its own, more suited to it, and indeed more pleasant.

13\. Constantly, and if possible with every impression that strikes the soul, apply to it the principles of natural philosophy, of ethics, and of logic.

14\. Whomever you meet, say to yourself at once: What opinions does this man hold about good and bad? If he holds such and such opinions about pleasure and pain and their causes, about fame and disgrace, about death and life, then it will not seem remarkable or strange to me if he does such and such things. And I will keep in mind that he is compelled to act as he does.

15\. Remember that it is shameful to be surprised when a fig tree produces figs, and equally shameful to be surprised when the world produces the things it is in its nature to produce. For a physician or a ship's pilot, it would be shameful to be surprised that a patient has a fever or that the wind is against him.

16\. Remember that to change your mind and follow someone who corrects your error is no less consistent with freedom than to persist in your error. For the action is your own, carried out according to your own impulse and judgment, and indeed according to your own understanding.

17\. If a thing is within your power, why do you do it? If it is in the power of someone else, whom do you blame: the atoms, that is, blind chance, or the gods? Either is foolish. You must blame no one. If you can, correct the cause; if you cannot, correct at least the thing itself; and if you cannot do even that, what use is fault-finding to you? Nothing should be done without a purpose.

18\. What has died does not drop out of the universe. If it stays here, it also changes here and dissolves into its proper parts, which are the elements of the universe and of yourself. And these too change, and they do not complain.

19\. Everything exists for some purpose, whether a horse or a vine. Why are you surprised? Even the sun will say, "I exist for a purpose," and the rest of the gods will say the same. For what purpose, then, do you exist? To enjoy pleasure? See whether common sense will allow that.

20\. In everything, nature has given no less thought to the end than to the beginning and the duration, like someone tossing a ball into the air. What good is it to the ball to be thrown up, or what harm to come down, or even to have fallen? What good is it to a bubble to hold together, or what harm to burst? The same may be said of a lamp's flame.

21\. Turn the body inside out and see what sort of thing it is. See what it becomes when it has grown old, and what it becomes when it is diseased.

Short-lived are both the one who praises and the one who is praised, the one who remembers and the one who is remembered. And all of this happens in a corner of this region of the world. Not even here does everyone agree, nor is any single person consistent with himself. And the whole earth itself is a mere point.

22\. Attend to the matter before you, whether it is an opinion, an act, or a word.

You deserve what you are suffering, for you would rather become good tomorrow than be good today.

23\. Am I doing something? I do it with reference to the good of humankind. Does something happen to me? I accept it and refer it to the gods and to the source of all things, from which everything that happens is derived.

24\. Think of how bathing appears to you: the oil, the sweat, the grime scraped from the skin, the filthy water, everything disgusting. Every part of life, and everything in it, is the same.

25\. Lucilla saw Verus die, and then Lucilla died. Secunda saw Maximus die, and then Secunda died. Epitynchanus saw Diotimus die, and then Epitynchanus died. Antoninus saw Faustina die, and then Antoninus died. Such is everything. Celer saw Hadrian die, and then Celer died. And those men of keen wit, whether seers or men puffed up with pride, where are they now? Where, for instance, are the sharp-witted Charax, and Demetrius the Platonist, and Eudaemon, and anyone else of their kind? All of them were creatures of a day, dead long ago. Some were not remembered even for a short while; others became the heroes of legends; and still others have faded even from legend. Remember this, then: this little compound that is yourself must either be dissolved, or your feeble breath must be extinguished, or else be removed and placed elsewhere.

26\. A person finds satisfaction in doing the work proper to a human being. And the work proper to a human being is to be benevolent toward one's own kind, to disregard the stirrings of the senses, to judge rightly among plausible appearances, and to survey the nature of the universe and the things that happen within it.

27\. You stand in three relations: one to the body that encloses you; a second to the divine cause from which all things come to all; and a third to those who live alongside you.

28\. Pain is an evil either to the body, in which case let the body say what it thinks of it, or to the soul. But the soul has the power to preserve its own serenity and calm, and to refuse to regard pain as an evil. For every judgment, every impulse, every desire and every aversion lies within, and no evil climbs that high.

29\. Wipe away your imaginings by often telling yourself: It is now in my power to allow no badness into this soul, no desire, no disturbance of any kind. Instead, looking at all things, I see their nature, and I use each according to its worth. Remember this power, which nature has given you.

30\. Whether you are in the Senate or speaking with anyone at all, speak fittingly and without affectation. Use plain speech.

31\. The court of Augustus: his wife, his daughter, his descendants, his ancestors, his sister, Agrippa, his kinsmen, his intimates, his friends; Areius the philosopher, Maecenas, his physicians and his sacrificing priests. The entire court is dead. Then turn to others, and consider not the death of a single man but the death of a whole family line, such as the Pompeii, and the words inscribed on tombs: *The last of his line.* Then reflect on how much trouble their forebears took to leave an heir, and on how, of necessity, someone must be the last. Here again, consider the death of an entire line.

32\. It is your duty to order your life well in every single action; and if each action fulfills its duty as far as possible, be content. No one can prevent each action from doing its duty.—But some external thing will stand in the way.—Nothing will stand in the way of your acting justly, soberly, and thoughtfully.—But perhaps some other active power will be hindered.—Very well. But if you accept the hindrance and contentedly turn your efforts to what is permitted, another opportunity for action immediately presents itself in place of the one that was blocked, and it will fit into the ordering of life we are speaking of.

33\. Accept wealth or prosperity without arrogance, and be ready to let it go.

34\. If you have ever seen a hand cut off, or a foot, or a head, lying somewhere apart from the rest of the body, then you have seen what a man makes of himself, so far as he can, when he is discontented with what happens, cuts himself off from others, or does anything unsocial. Suppose you have detached yourself from the natural unity: you were made by nature as a part, and now you have severed yourself from it. Yet there is this beautiful provision, that it remains in your power to unite yourself once more. God has granted this to no other part: to come back together after it has been separated and cut away. Consider, then, the kindness with which he has distinguished humankind. He has placed it in a person's power never to be separated from the whole at all, and, once separated, to return, to be reunited, and to resume his place as a part.

35\. Just as the nature of the whole has given every rational being all the other powers it possesses, so we have received this power from it as well. Universal nature takes whatever stands in its way and resists it, turns it to its own purpose, fixes it in its destined place, and makes it part of itself. In the same way, the rational creature can make every obstacle into material for its own use, and turn it to whatever purpose it may have intended.

36\. Do not unsettle yourself by contemplating your life as a whole. Do not let your thoughts take in, all at once, the many troubles you expect may befall you. Instead, on each occasion, ask yourself: What is there in this that is unbearable, beyond all enduring? You will be ashamed to admit that there is anything. Next, remember that neither the future nor the past weighs on you, only the present. And the present shrinks to very little if you simply mark out its limits, and reproach your mind if it cannot hold out against even this much.

37\. Do Panthea or Pergamus still sit beside the tomb of Verus? Do Chaurias or Diotimus sit beside the tomb of Hadrian? That would be absurd. Well, suppose they did sit there: would the dead be aware of it? And if the dead were aware, would they be pleased? And if they were pleased, would that make them immortal? Was it not ordained by destiny that these mourners too should first grow into old women and old men and then die? And once they were dead, what would those others do then? All of this is stench and blood in a bag.

38\. "If you have keen sight, look and judge wisely," says the philosopher.

39\. In the constitution of the rational creature I see no virtue that opposes justice; but I do see a virtue that opposes the love of pleasure, and that is temperance.

40\. If you remove your opinion about whatever seems to cause you pain, you yourself stand in perfect security.—And who is this self?—Reason.—But I am not reason.—So be it. Then let reason, at least, not trouble itself. And if some other part of you is suffering, let that part form its own opinion about itself.

41\. Any obstruction of the senses' perceptions is an evil to the animal nature. Any obstruction of its impulses and desires is likewise an evil to the animal nature. In the same way, something else is an obstruction and an evil to the constitution of plants. It follows that whatever obstructs the intelligence is an evil to the intelligent nature. Apply all of this to yourself. Does pain or sensual pleasure affect you? The senses will see to that. Has some obstacle stood against you in your efforts toward a goal? If you were pursuing that goal absolutely, unconditionally and without reservation, then this obstacle is indeed an evil to you as a rational being. But if you took into account the ordinary course of things, you have not yet been harmed, nor even hindered. As for the things proper to the understanding, no one else is in the habit of obstructing them, for neither fire, nor iron, nor tyrant, nor abuse touches the understanding in any way. Once it has become a sphere, perfectly rounded and complete in itself, it remains a sphere.

42\. It is not right that I should cause myself pain, for I have never intentionally caused pain even to another.

43\. Different things delight different people. My delight is to keep my ruling faculty sound, turning away neither from any person nor from anything that befalls people, but looking upon all things and receiving them with welcoming eyes, and using each according to its worth.

44\. Make sure you secure this present moment for yourself. Those who chase fame after death forget that the people of later times will be exactly like the people they cannot stand now, and that both alike are mortal. And what does it matter to you in any way if those later people make this or that sound, or hold this or that opinion of you?

45\. Take me and cast me wherever you will, for there I shall keep my divine part tranquil, which is to say content, so long as it can feel and act in keeping with its own proper constitution. Is this change of place sufficient reason for my soul to be unhappy and worse than it was: cast down, grasping, shrinking back, terrified? And what could you find that would be sufficient reason for this?

46\. Nothing can happen to any human being that is not a human occurrence, nor to an ox anything not in keeping with the nature of an ox, nor to a vine anything not in keeping with the nature of a vine, nor to a stone anything not proper to a stone. If, then, what happens to each thing is both usual and natural, why should you complain? Common nature brings nothing that you cannot bear.

47\. If some external thing pains you, it is not the thing itself that disturbs you but your own judgment about it, and it is in your power to wipe out that judgment now. But if something in your own disposition pains you, who is stopping you from correcting your opinion? And even if you are pained because you are not doing some particular thing that seems right to you, why not act rather than complain?—But some insurmountable obstacle stands in the way.—Then do not grieve, for the reason it is not being done does not depend on you.—But life is not worth living if this cannot be done.—Then depart from life contentedly, just as a man dies who is in the midst of full activity, and well pleased, too, with the very things that stand in his way.

48\. Remember that the ruling faculty is invincible when, gathered into itself, it is satisfied with itself and does nothing it does not choose to do, even if its resistance springs from sheer obstinacy. How much more invincible will it be when it forms a judgment about anything deliberately and with the aid of reason? The mind free of passions is therefore a citadel, for a human being has no more secure refuge to flee to, where he may remain impregnable from then on. Whoever has not seen this is ignorant; but whoever has seen it and does not flee to this refuge is unhappy.

49\. Tell yourself nothing beyond what first impressions report. Suppose you are told that a certain person is speaking ill of you. That much has been reported; but that you have been harmed has not been reported. I see that my child is sick. That I do see; but that he is in danger, I do not see. Always keep, then, to first impressions, and add nothing of your own from within, and nothing happens to you. Or rather, add something, but do so as one who knows everything that happens in the world.

50\. The cucumber is bitter?—Throw it away.—There are brambles in the road?—Step around them.—That is enough. Do not go on to ask why such things were ever made in the world. A student of nature would laugh at you, just as a carpenter or a shoemaker would laugh if you found fault because you saw shavings and scraps from their work lying about the shop. And yet they have places where they can throw these shavings and scraps, whereas universal nature has no space outside itself. The marvel of her craft is that, though she has set limits around herself, she transforms into herself everything within her that seems to decay, to grow old, and to be useless, and from these very same materials makes other new things. So she needs neither substance from outside nor a place to cast off what decays. She is content, then, with her own space, her own matter, and her own art.

51\. Do not be sluggish in your actions, nor disorderly in your conversation, nor wandering in your thoughts. Let there be neither inner strife in your soul nor outward overflow, and do not be so busy in life that you have no leisure.

Suppose people kill you, cut you to pieces, curse you. What can any of this do to keep your mind from remaining pure, wise, sober, and just? Imagine a man standing beside a clear, pure spring and cursing it: the spring never stops sending up drinkable water. And if he throws mud or filth into it, the spring will quickly disperse it and wash it away, and will not be polluted in the least. How, then, can you possess a perpetual fountain rather than a mere well? By shaping yourself, hour by hour, toward freedom joined with contentment, simplicity, and modesty.

52\. Whoever does not know what the world is does not know where he is. And whoever does not know the purpose for which the world exists does not know who he is, or what the world is. But anyone who has failed in even one of these things could not say for what purpose he himself exists. What, then, do you think of a man who seeks, or shuns, the praise of those who applaud, people who know neither where they are nor who they are?

53\. Do you want to be praised by a man who curses himself three times an hour? Do you want to please a man who does not please himself? And does a man please himself who regrets nearly everything he does?

54\. Let your breathing no longer be the only part of you in concert with the surrounding air; let your intelligence, too, now be in harmony with the intelligence that embraces all things. For that intelligent power is diffused through every part and pervades all things for anyone willing to draw it in, no less than the air does for anyone able to breathe it.

55\. In general, wickedness does no harm at all to the universe; and in particular, one person's wickedness does no harm to another. It harms only the one who has it in his power to be free of it the moment he chooses.

56\. To my own free will, my neighbor's free will is as much a matter of indifference as his feeble breath and flesh. For although we were made above all for one another's sake, the ruling faculty of each of us has its own proper office. Otherwise my neighbor's wickedness would be my harm, and God did not will that, so that my unhappiness should not depend on someone else.

57\. The sun's light seems to pour down, and it is indeed diffused in every direction, yet it does not pour out and run off. This diffusion is extension, which is why its rays are called *aktines*, "extensions," on the grounds that they are extended, *ekteinesthai* in Greek. You can judge what sort of thing a ray is by watching sunlight enter a darkened room through a narrow opening. It stretches in a straight line, and is, as it were, broken off when it meets some solid body that stands in its path and cuts off the air beyond; yet there the light stays fixed, and does not slip away or fall. The understanding should pour itself out and spread in the same way. It should never be a spilling out but always an extension. It should not strike violently or rashly against the obstacles in its path, yet neither should it fall away; it should stay fixed and illuminate whatever receives it. A body that refuses to admit the light deprives itself of illumination.

58\. Whoever fears death fears either the loss of sensation or a different kind of sensation. But if you have no sensation, you will feel no harm; and if you acquire another kind of sensation, you will be a different kind of living being, and you will not cease to live.

59\. People exist for one another's sake. Teach them, then, or bear with them.

60\. An arrow moves one way, the mind another. Yet the mind, whether it is exercising caution or engaged in inquiry, moves straight ahead all the same, toward its object.

61\. Enter into every person's ruling faculty, and let every other person enter into yours.

## Book IX

1\. Whoever acts unjustly acts impiously. Universal nature made rational creatures for one another's sake, to help each other according to their deserts and in no way to harm one another, so whoever transgresses her will is plainly guilty of impiety toward the highest divinity. Whoever lies is guilty of impiety toward the same divinity. For universal nature is the nature of the things that are, and the things that are stand in relation to everything that comes into being. Moreover, this universal nature is called truth, and it is the first cause of all things that are true. The one who lies intentionally, then, is guilty of impiety, since he acts unjustly by deceiving. The one who lies unintentionally is guilty as well, since he is at odds with universal nature and disturbs its order by fighting against the nature of the world. Whoever is carried of his own accord toward what is contrary to truth is fighting against it, for he received from nature the faculties for telling falsehood from truth, and through neglecting them he can no longer do so. Likewise, whoever pursues pleasure as a good and shuns pain as an evil is guilty of impiety. Such a person must necessarily find fault with universal nature again and again, accusing it of allotting things to the bad and the good contrary to what they deserve, since the bad often enjoy pleasure and possess what produces it, while the good meet with pain and the things that cause it. Further, whoever fears pain will at times also fear some of the things that are going to happen in the world, and this too is impiety. And whoever pursues pleasure will not hold back from injustice, which is plainly impiety. Now, there are things toward which universal nature is equally disposed, for it would not have made both of a pair unless it were equally disposed toward both. Toward these, those who wish to follow nature should be of one mind with it and equally disposed as well. So with respect to pain and pleasure, death and life, honor and dishonor, all of which universal nature employs equally, whoever is not equally disposed toward them is manifestly acting impiously. When I say that universal nature employs them equally, I mean that they happen alike to things produced in continuous succession and to those that come after them. This occurs by virtue of a certain original impulse of Providence, by which it moved from a certain beginning toward this present ordering of things, having conceived certain principles of what was to be, and having fixed the powers that generate beings, changes, and successions of this kind.

2\. The happiest lot would be to leave humankind without ever having tasted falsehood, hypocrisy, luxury, or pride. But to breathe one's last after having had one's fill of these things is, as the proverb has it, the next-best voyage, the course a sailor takes when the first is closed to him. Have you resolved to remain with vice? Has experience not yet persuaded you to flee from this plague? For the corruption of the understanding is a plague, far more so than any such tainting and alteration of the air that surrounds us. That corruption of the air is a plague to living creatures insofar as they are animals; the other is a plague to human beings insofar as they are human.

3\. Do not scorn death; be content with it, since this too is one of the things nature wills. Think of what it is to be young and to grow old, to grow and reach maturity, to cut teeth and grow a beard and gray hairs, to beget, to conceive, and to give birth, and all the other natural processes that the seasons of your life bring. Dissolution is just such a process. It befits a thoughtful person, then, to be neither careless nor impatient nor contemptuous toward death, but to await it as one of nature's operations. As you now wait for the moment when the child will come forth from your wife's womb, so be ready for the moment when your soul will slip out of this casing. But if you also want a commonplace sort of comfort, one that will reach your heart, you will be best reconciled to death by considering the things from which you are about to be parted, and the characters of the people with whom your soul will no longer be mingled. It is by no means right to take offense at people; your duty is to care for them and bear with them gently. Yet remember that your departure will not be from people who share your principles. That is the only thing, if anything, that could pull us the other way and bind us to life: being allowed to live among those who hold the same principles as ourselves. But as it is, you see how much trouble comes from discord among those who live together, so that you might say: Come quickly, O death, lest I too perhaps forget myself.

4\. Whoever does wrong does wrong against himself. Whoever acts unjustly acts unjustly toward himself, because he makes himself bad.

5\. A person often acts unjustly by failing to do something, not only by doing something.

6\. Your present judgment grounded in understanding, your present conduct directed toward the common good, and your present disposition of contentment with all that happens: that is enough.

7\. Wipe out imagination. Check desire. Extinguish appetite. Keep the ruling faculty in its own power.

8\. Among the animals that lack reason, a single life is distributed; among rational animals, a single intelligent soul is distributed. In the same way, there is one earth for all things of an earthly nature, and we see by one light and breathe one air, all of us who have the faculty of sight and all who have life.

9\. All things that share in something common to them all are drawn toward whatever is of their own kind. Everything earthy tends toward the earth, everything liquid flows together, and everything airy does the same, so that something must keep them apart, and force must be applied to do it. Fire rises because of the elemental fire above, yet it is so ready to kindle together with all the fire here below that any substance with even a little dryness in it catches easily, because less is mixed into it of whatever hinders burning. In the same way, then, everything that shares in the common intelligent nature is drawn toward its own kind, and even more strongly. The more it surpasses all other things, the readier it is to mingle and fuse with what is akin to it. So among animals without reason we find swarms of bees, herds of cattle, the rearing of nestlings, and something like love. Even animals have souls, and the force that draws them together shows itself in a higher degree than has ever been observed in plants, stones, or trees. Among rational animals there are political communities and friendships, households and public gatherings, and in war there are treaties and truces. Among beings higher still, a kind of unity exists even when they are separated from one another, as among the stars. Thus the ascent to a higher level can produce a sympathy even between things that lie far apart. But look at what is happening now: only intelligent creatures have forgotten this mutual desire and inclination, and in them alone the tendency to flow together is no longer seen. Yet even as men try to escape this union, they are caught and held by it, for their nature is too strong for them, and you will see the truth of this if you only watch. You will sooner find something earthy that touches nothing earthy than a man wholly cut off from other men.

10\. Man, God, and the universe all bear fruit, each in its proper season. That custom has attached the word especially to the vine and plants like it makes no difference. Reason, too, bears fruit, both for all and for itself, and from it grow other things of the same kind as reason itself.

11\. If you can, correct wrongdoers by teaching them. If you cannot, remember that your capacity for forbearance was given to you for exactly this purpose. The gods, too, are forbearing toward such people, and for some purposes they even help them to health, wealth, and reputation, so kind are they. You can do the same. Or tell me, who prevents you?

12\. Do not labor like someone wretched, nor like someone who wants to be pitied or admired. Direct your will toward one thing only: to set yourself in motion and to hold yourself back as social reason requires.

13\. Today I escaped from all trouble. Or rather, I threw all trouble out, for it was never outside me. It was within, in my own opinions.

14\. All things are the same: familiar in experience, fleeting in time, worthless in substance. Everything now is just as it was in the days of those we have buried.

15\. Things stand outside us, existing by themselves, knowing nothing of themselves and passing no judgment. What, then, judges them? The ruling faculty.

16\. The evil and the good of a rational, social creature lie not in what he undergoes but in what he does, just as his virtue and his vice lie not in being acted upon but in acting.

17\. For a stone thrown into the air, it is no evil to fall back down, nor was it any good to have been carried up.

18\. Look deep into the governing principles of men, and you will see what judges you are afraid of, and what sort of judges they are of themselves.

19\. All things are changing. You yourself are in continuous transformation, and in a sense in continuous destruction, and so is the whole universe.

20\. Your duty is to leave another man's wrongdoing where it lies.

21\. The ending of an activity, the cessation of movement and opinion, which is in a sense their death, is no evil. Turn your thoughts now to your own life: your life as a child, as a youth, as a grown man, as an old man. Each of these changes was a death. Was that anything to fear? Turn your thoughts next to your life under your grandfather, then under your mother, then under your father. As you find many other differences, changes, and endings, ask yourself: Is this anything to fear? In the same way, then, the ending, cessation, and change of your whole life is nothing to be afraid of either.

22\. Hasten to examine your own ruling faculty, that of the universe, and that of your neighbor. Examine your own so that you may make it just; that of the universe so that you may remember what you are a part of; and your neighbor's so that you may know whether he acted in ignorance or with knowledge, and may also bear in mind that his ruling faculty is akin to yours.

23\. Just as you are a component part of a social system, let every act of yours be a component part of social life. Any act of yours that bears no relation, near or remote, to a social end tears your life apart and keeps it from being whole. It is a kind of mutiny, like a man in a public assembly who stands off by himself, apart from the common agreement.

24\. Children's quarrels and their games, and little souls carrying corpses about: such is everything. And so what is shown in depictions of the realm of the dead strikes our eyes all the more clearly.

25\. Examine the quality of a thing's form, separate it entirely from its material part, and contemplate it. Then determine the longest time that a thing of this particular form is naturally made to last.

26\. You have suffered endless troubles because you were not content to let your ruling faculty do what it is naturally constituted to do. But enough of this.

27\. When someone blames you or hates you, or when people say anything injurious about you, approach their poor souls, go inside them, and see what sort of men they are. You will find no reason to trouble yourself over what these people think of you. Still, you must be well disposed toward them, for by nature they are your friends. And the gods, too, help them in every way, by dreams and by signs, toward the things they value.

28\. The cycles of the universe are always the same, rising and falling from age to age. Either the universal intelligence sets itself in motion for each separate effect, and if so, accept whatever results from its activity; or it moved once, and everything else follows from that in sequence; or indivisible atoms are the origin of all things. In a word: if there is a god, all is well; and if chance rules, do not let yourself be ruled by chance as well.

Soon the earth will cover us all. Then the earth itself will change, and whatever results from that change will go on changing forever, and that again forever. Whoever reflects on these changes and transformations, following one upon another like wave upon wave, and on how swiftly they come, will despise everything perishable.

29\. The universal cause is like a winter torrent: it sweeps everything along with it. And how worthless are all these little men who busy themselves with politics and imagine they are acting as philosophers! Drivelers, every one. Well then, man, do what nature now requires. Set yourself in motion, if it is in your power, and do not look around to see whether anyone will notice. Do not hope for the perfect state of Plato's *Republic*, but be content if the smallest thing goes well, and consider even that no small achievement. For who can change men's opinions? And without a change of opinion, what is there but the slavery of men who groan while pretending to obey? Come now, tell me about Alexander, and Philip, and Demetrius of Phalerum. They themselves must judge whether they understood what the common nature required and trained themselves accordingly. But if they strutted like heroes in a tragedy, no one has condemned me to imitate them. The work of philosophy is simple and modest. Do not lead me astray into arrogance and pride.

30\. Look down from above on the countless herds of men and their countless rituals, their endlessly varied voyages through storm and calm, and the differences among those who are born, who live together, and who die. Consider, too, the lives that others lived long ago, the lives of those who will come after you, and the lives now being lived among barbarian peoples. Consider how many do not even know your name, how many will soon forget it, and how those who perhaps praise you now will very soon blame you; and consider that neither posthumous fame, nor reputation, nor anything else of the kind has any value.

31\. Let there be freedom from disturbance in regard to what comes from the external cause, and justice in what you do by virtue of the internal cause. That is, let your impulses and actions find their end in social acts, for this accords with your nature.

32\. You can clear away many of the useless things that disturb you, for they lie entirely in your opinion. You will then gain ample room for yourself by embracing the whole universe in your mind, by contemplating the eternity of time, and by observing how rapidly each separate thing changes: how short the time from birth to dissolution, how boundless the time before birth, and how equally boundless the time after dissolution.

33\. Everything you see will soon perish, and those who watched it perish will very soon perish too. The man who dies in the most extreme old age will be brought to the same condition as the man who died young.

34\. What are these men's governing principles? What sort of things are they busy about, and for what reasons do they love and honor? Picture their poor souls laid bare. When they imagine that they do harm by their blame or good by their praise, what a notion!

35\. Loss is nothing but change. But universal nature delights in change, and in obedience to her all things are now done well. From eternity they have taken the same form, and they will continue so for time without end. What, then, do you say? That all things have been bad and always will be bad? That among so many gods no power has ever been found to set them right, and that the world has been condemned to be bound in unceasing evil?

36\. How rotten is the matter that underlies everything! Water, dust, bones, filth. Or again: marble, a mere callus of the earth; gold and silver, its sediments; clothing, nothing but bits of hair; purple dye, the blood of a shellfish; and everything else is of the same kind. And the breath of life is another such thing, passing from this to that.

37\. Enough of this miserable life, this grumbling, this apish mimicry. Why are you disturbed? What is new in this? What unsettles you? Is it the form of the thing? Look at it. Or is it the matter? Look at that. Besides these there is nothing. As for the gods, then, become at last simpler and better toward them. It is all the same whether we study these things for a hundred years or for three.

38\. If a man has done wrong, the harm is his own. But perhaps he has not done wrong.

39\. Either all things spring from a single intelligent source and come together as in one body, in which case the part ought not to find fault with what is done for the good of the whole; or there are only atoms, and nothing but mingling and scattering. Why, then, are you disturbed? Say to your ruling faculty: Are you dead? Are you corrupted? Are you playing the hypocrite? Have you become a beast, herding and grazing with the rest?

40\. Either the gods have no power or they have power. If they have no power, why do you pray to them? But if they have power, why not pray that they give you the ability not to fear any of the things you fear, not to desire any of the things you desire, and not to be pained by anything, rather than praying that this or that thing should happen or not happen? Surely, if they can help men at all, they can help them toward these ends. But perhaps you will say that the gods have placed these things in your own power. Well, then, is it not better to use what is in your power like a free man than to long in a slavish and abject way for what is not? And who told you that the gods do not help us even in the things that are in our power? Begin, then, to pray for such things, and you will see. One man prays: "How may I sleep with that woman?" You should pray: "How may I stop desiring to sleep with her?" Another prays: "How may I be rid of this?" You should pray: "How may I stop wishing to be rid of it?" Another prays: "How may I keep from losing my little son?" You should pray: "How may I stop being afraid of losing him?" In short, turn your prayers this way, and see what comes of it.

41\. Epicurus says: "During my illness I did not talk about my bodily sufferings, nor did I discuss such things with those who came to see me. I went on discussing the nature of things as before, keeping to this main point: how the mind, while sharing in the disturbances that pass through our poor flesh, may remain free of agitation and keep hold of its proper good. Nor did I give the doctors a chance to put on solemn faces, as if they were doing something important. My life went on well and happily." Do as he did, then, both in sickness, if you fall sick, and in any other circumstance. Never to abandon philosophy whatever befalls us, and never to engage in idle chatter with an ignorant man or with one who knows nothing of nature, is a principle shared by every school of philosophy. Be intent only on what you are doing now and on the instrument by which you do it.

42\. When you are offended by someone's shameless behavior, ask yourself at once: Is it possible that there should be no shameless people in the world? It is not possible. Do not, then, demand the impossible. This man is simply one of the shameless people who must necessarily exist in the world. Keep the same thought ready in the case of the scoundrel, the faithless man, and anyone who does wrong in any way. For as soon as you remind yourself that such people cannot fail to exist, you will become more kindly disposed toward each of them individually. It is useful, too, to consider at once, when the occasion arises, what virtue nature has given man to set against each kind of wrong. Against the stupid man she has given mildness as an antidote, and against other kinds of men other powers. In every case you can correct by teaching the man who has gone astray, for everyone who does wrong misses his aim and has gone astray. Besides, how have you been harmed? You will find that none of those who irritate you has done anything that could make your mind worse, and it is only in your mind that whatever is evil or harmful to you has its foundation. What harm is done, or what is so strange, if an untaught man does what untaught men do? Consider whether you should not rather blame yourself for failing to expect that such a man would go wrong in such a way. Your reason gave you the means to see that he was likely to commit this error, yet you forgot, and now you are astonished that he has erred. Above all, when you blame a man as faithless or ungrateful, turn your attention to yourself. The fault is plainly yours, either because you trusted a man of such a character to keep his promise, or because, when you did him a kindness, you did not do it unconditionally, and in such a way that the act itself gave you all the reward you needed. What more do you want when you have done someone a service? Is it not enough that you have acted in accordance with your nature? Must you be paid for it too? That is as if the eye demanded payment for seeing, or the feet for walking. These parts of the body are formed for a particular purpose, and by working according to their own constitutions they receive what is properly theirs. In the same way, man is formed by nature for acts of kindness, and when he has done something kind, or otherwise served the common good, he has acted in accordance with his constitution, and he has received what is properly his.

## Book X

1\. My soul, will you never be good and simple and whole and naked, more plainly visible than the body that surrounds you? Will you never know an affectionate and contented disposition? Will you never be full and lacking nothing, longing for nothing more and desiring nothing, animate or inanimate, for the enjoyment of pleasure? Will you never stop wanting more time in which to prolong your enjoyment, or a better place, or a pleasant climate, or the company of people with whom you might live in harmony? Will you not instead be satisfied with your present condition and pleased with everything around you? Will you not convince yourself that you have everything, that it all comes from the gods, that all is well with you and will be well, whatever pleases them and whatever they give for the preservation of the perfect living being, which is good and just and beautiful, which generates and holds together all things, and which contains and embraces all things that are dissolved so that others like them may be produced? Will you never be such that you live in fellowship with gods and men, neither finding fault with them at all nor being condemned by them?

2\. Observe what your nature requires insofar as you are governed by nature alone, as a growing thing. Then do it and accept it, provided that your nature as a living being will not be made worse by it. Next, observe what your nature requires insofar as you are a living being. All of this you may allow yourself, provided that your nature as a rational animal will not be made worse by it. And a rational animal is, by consequence, also a political, that is, a social, animal. Follow these rules, then, and trouble yourself about nothing else.

3\. Everything that happens either happens in a way that you are naturally formed to bear, or in a way that you are not. If it happens in a way you are naturally formed to bear, do not complain; bear it as nature has formed you to. If it happens in a way you are not naturally formed to bear, do not complain either, for it will perish after it has consumed you. Remember, however, that you are naturally formed to bear everything that your own opinion can make endurable and tolerable, by regarding it as either in your interest or your duty to do so.

4\. If someone is mistaken, instruct him kindly and show him his error. If you cannot, blame yourself, or not even yourself.

5\. Whatever happens to you was prepared for you from all eternity, and the web of causes has been spinning, from eternity, the thread of your existence and of everything that befalls it.

6\. Whether the universe is a random collection of atoms or nature is an ordered system, let this first be settled: I am a part of the whole that nature governs. Next, I am in a sense closely related to the other parts that are of the same kind as myself. If I remember this, then insofar as I am a part, I shall be discontented with nothing assigned to me by the whole, for nothing that benefits the whole can injure the part. The whole contains nothing that is not to its own advantage. All natures share this principle, but the nature of the universe has this further principle: no external cause can compel it to produce anything harmful to itself. By remembering, then, that I am a part of such a whole, I shall be content with everything that happens. And insofar as I am closely related to the parts that are of the same kind as myself, I shall do nothing unsocial. I shall turn instead toward those who share my nature, direct all my efforts to the common interest, and divert them from whatever opposes it. If things are done in this way, life must flow on happily, just as you can see that a citizen's life is happy when he pursues a course of action that benefits his fellow citizens and is content with whatever the state assigns to him.

7\. The parts of the whole, by which I mean everything naturally contained in the universe, must of necessity perish. But let this be understood to mean that they must undergo change. Now, if this were by nature both an evil and a necessity for the parts, the whole could not remain in good condition, since its parts are subject to change and constituted to perish in various ways. For did Nature herself set out to do harm to the things that are parts of her, to make them liable to evil and bound by necessity to fall into it? Or did such results come about without her knowledge?

Both of these suppositions are, in fact, incredible. But even if a man were to drop the term "Nature," in the sense of an active, efficient power, and speak of these things simply as natural, it would still be absurd to assert that the parts of the whole are by their very nature subject to change and, in the same breath, to be surprised or upset as though something were happening contrary to nature. This is all the more absurd because things dissolve into the very elements of which each was composed. Either the elements from which a thing was compounded are dispersed, or there is a change, the solid passing into earth and the airy into air, so that these parts are taken back into the universal reason, whether that reason is consumed by fire at certain periods or renewed through eternal changes. And do not imagine that the solid and airy parts of you have belonged to you since the moment you were born. All of this was added only yesterday or the day before, so to speak, from the food you ate and the air you breathed. What changes, then, is this accumulated material, not what your mother brought into the world. And suppose that this original part does bind you very closely to that other part, the one whose distinctive quality is change. Even so, this is no real objection to what has been said.

8\. Once you have taken on these names (good, modest, truthful, rational, even-tempered, and magnanimous), take care that you do not exchange them for others, and if you should lose them, return to them quickly. Remember that the term "rational" was meant to signify a discerning attention to each individual thing and a freedom from carelessness. "Equanimity" means the willing acceptance of whatever the common nature assigns to you. "Magnanimity" means raising the intelligent part of yourself above the pleasant or painful sensations of the flesh, above that paltry thing called fame, above death, and above everything of that kind. If, then, you keep yourself in possession of these names, without wanting others to call you by them, you will become another person and enter upon another life. To go on being what you have been until now, torn to pieces and defiled in such a life, is the mark of a very stupid man, one far too attached to living. He is like those half-eaten fighters in the arena who, covered with wounds and gore, still beg to be kept alive until the next day, though they will be thrown, in that same condition, to the same claws and the same teeth. Fix yourself, therefore, in possession of these few names. If you are able to remain in them, remain there as though you had been carried off to the Islands of the Blessed. But if you find that you are slipping out of them and cannot keep your hold, go bravely into some quiet corner where you can keep them. Or else depart from life at once, not in anger but with simplicity, freedom, and modesty, having done at least this one praiseworthy thing in your life: to have left it in such a manner. To help you remember these names, it will be of great use to remember the gods, and that they do not wish to be flattered but wish all rational beings to become like themselves. Remember too that what does the work of a fig tree is a fig tree, what does the work of a dog is a dog, what does the work of a bee is a bee, and what does the work of a man is a man.

9\. Mimes, war, bewilderment, torpor, and servitude will wipe out those sacred principles of yours day after day. How many things do you imagine without studying nature, and how many do you neglect? Yet it is your duty to look at everything and do everything so that, all at once, your capacity for dealing with circumstances is perfected and your power of contemplation is exercised. You must also keep up the confidence that comes from knowing each individual thing, neither displaying it nor hiding it. For when will you enjoy simplicity? When will you enjoy seriousness? When will you know each individual thing: what it is in substance, what place it holds in the universe, how long it is formed to last, what it is composed of, to whom it can belong, and who is able both to give it and to take it away?

10\. A spider is proud when it has caught a fly. One man is proud when he has caught a wretched hare, another when he has netted a little fish, another when he has taken wild boars, another bears, another Sarmatians. If you examine their principles, are these not all robbers?

11\. Acquire the habit of contemplating how all things change into one another. Attend to it constantly, and train yourself in this branch of philosophy, for nothing is so well suited to producing greatness of mind. Such a man has already put off the body. He sees that he must depart from among men and leave everything here behind, and that no one knows how soon. So he devotes himself entirely to acting justly in everything he does, and in everything else that happens he surrenders himself to universal nature. What anyone may say or think about him, or do against him, never even crosses his mind. He is content with two things: acting justly in what he is doing now, and being satisfied with what is now assigned to him. He sets aside all distracting and busy pursuits and wants nothing except to travel the straight path through the law, and by traveling that straight path, to follow God.

12\. What need is there for suspicious fear, when it is in your power to inquire what ought to be done? If you see clearly, go that way contentedly, without turning back. If you do not see clearly, stop and consult the best advisers. And if other obstacles stand in your way, press on as far as your powers allow, with due deliberation, holding to what appears to be just. To reach this goal is best, and if you do fail, let your failure come in the attempt. The man who follows reason in all things is at once tranquil and active, and cheerful and composed as well.

13\. As soon as you wake from sleep, ask yourself whether it will make any difference to you if another person does what is just and right. It will make no difference.

You have not forgotten, I suppose, that those who put on arrogant airs while handing out praise or blame to others are the very same people at their beds and at their tables. You have not forgotten what they do, what they avoid, and what they pursue, or how they steal and rob, not with hands and feet but with the most precious part of themselves. That part, when a man so chooses, brings forth fidelity, modesty, truth, law, and a good daemon, which is to say happiness.

14\. To nature, who gives all things and takes them all back, the educated and modest man says: Give what you will; take back what you will. And he says this not out of pride, but in obedience, and well pleased with her.

15\. Short is the little that remains to you of life. Live as though on a mountain. It makes no difference whether a man lives there or here, provided that wherever he lives in the world, he lives as a citizen of a political community. Let men see, let them know, a real man who lives according to nature. If they cannot bear him, let them kill him. That is better than living as they do.

16\. Stop talking at all about what kind of man a good man ought to be, and be one.

17\. Constantly contemplate the whole of time and the whole of substance. Consider that every individual thing, measured against all substance, is a fig seed, and measured against all time, a single turn of a gimlet, the small hand tool used to bore holes in wood.

18\. Look at everything that exists and observe that it is already dissolving and changing, in a state of decay or dispersal, as it were, or that everything is so constituted by nature as to die.

19\. Consider what men are like when they are eating, sleeping, copulating, relieving themselves, and so on. Then consider what kind of men they are when they are domineering and arrogant, or angry and scolding from their lofty positions. Only a short while ago, how many masters did they serve as slaves, and for what ends? And consider what condition they will be in a little while from now.

20\. Whatever universal nature brings to each thing is for that thing's good. And it is for its good at the very time nature brings it.

21\. "The earth loves the shower," and "the solemn ether loves," and the universe loves to make whatever is about to come into being. I say, then, to the universe: I love as you love. And is this not also what is meant when people say that this or that "loves," that is, is wont, to happen?

22\. Either you go on living here, and are already used to it, or you are going away, and that was your own choice, or you are dying, and have finished your service. Beyond these there is nothing. So take heart.

23\. Let this always be clear to you: this piece of land is like any other, and everything here is the same as things on a mountaintop, or on the seashore, or wherever you choose to be. You will find exactly what Plato describes: dwelling within the walls of a city as in a shepherd's fold on a mountain.

24\. What is my ruling faculty to me now? What am I making of it at this moment, and for what purpose am I using it? Is it empty of understanding? Has it been cut loose and torn away from social life? Has it melted into the poor flesh and become so mixed with it that it moves along with it?

25\. A slave who flees his master is a runaway. But the law is a master, and whoever breaks the law is a runaway. So too is the person who is grieved, angry, or afraid, because he is dissatisfied that something has happened, is happening, or will happen among the things appointed by the one who rules all things. That ruler is Law, and he assigns to every person what is fitting. Whoever fears, grieves, or is angry, then, is a runaway.

26\. A man deposits seed in a womb and goes away, and then another cause takes it up, labors on it, and makes a child. What a thing to come from such material! Again, the child passes food down its throat, and then another cause takes it and produces perception and motion, and in short, life and strength and much else besides. How many things, and how strange! Observe, then, the things that are produced in such a hidden way, and see the power at work in them, just as we see the power that carries things downward and upward. We do not see it with our eyes, but we see it no less plainly.

27\. Consider constantly how everything as it now exists also existed in the past, and consider that it will be the same again. Set before your eyes entire dramas and stages of the same form, whatever you have learned from your own experience or from older history: for example, the whole court of Hadrian, the whole court of Antoninus Pius, and the whole courts of Philip, Alexander, and Croesus. All of these were dramas like the ones we see now, only with different actors.

28\. Imagine everyone who is grieved or discontented at anything to be like a pig being sacrificed, kicking and squealing.

The man who lies on his bed in silence, lamenting the bonds that hold us, is like this pig too. And consider that only the rational animal has been given the power to follow willingly what happens. Simply to follow is a necessity imposed on all.

29\. On the occasion of each separate thing you do, pause and ask yourself whether death is a dreadful thing because it deprives you of this.

30\. When you are offended by someone's fault, turn at once to yourself and reflect on the ways in which you go wrong in a similar manner: for example, in thinking that money is a good thing, or pleasure, or a bit of reputation, and the like. By attending to this you will quickly forget your anger, especially if you add the further consideration that the man is under compulsion. What else could he do? Or, if you are able, remove the compulsion from him.

31\. When you have seen Satyron the Socratic, think of Eutyches or Hymen. When you have seen Euphrates, think of Eutychion or Silvanus. When you have seen Alciphron, think of Tropaeophorus. When you have seen Xenophon, think of Crito or Severus. When you have looked at yourself, think of any other Caesar, and do the same in the case of every person. Then let this thought be in your mind: Where are those men now? Nowhere, or nobody knows where. In this way you will continually look on human things as smoke and nothing at all, especially if you reflect at the same time that what has once changed will never exist again in the infinite duration of time. And you, in how brief a span does your existence lie? Why are you not content to pass through this short time in an orderly way? What material and what opportunity for your activity are you running away from? For what else are all these things except exercises for the reason, once it has looked carefully at the things that happen in life and examined their nature? Persevere, then, until you have made these things your own, as a strong stomach makes every food its own, and as a blazing fire makes flame and brightness out of whatever is thrown into it.

32\. Let no one have the power to say truthfully of you that you are not simple, or that you are not good. Let whoever thinks anything of the kind about you be a liar, and this is entirely within your power. For who can prevent you from being good and simple? Only resolve to live no longer unless you are such a person. Reason itself does not allow you to live if you are not.

33\. Given the material of our life, what can be done or said in the way most in keeping with reason? Whatever that may be, it is in your power to do it or say it, so do not make excuses that you are hindered. You will not stop lamenting until your mind reaches this condition: that what luxury is to those who enjoy pleasure, the doing of things in keeping with a human being's constitution will be to you, in whatever material is set before you and placed at your disposal. A person ought to regard as an enjoyment everything it is in his power to do according to his own nature, and it is in his power everywhere. A cylinder is not given the power to move everywhere by its own motion; it rolls only where the ground slopes. Neither is water, nor fire, nor anything else governed by nature or by an irrational soul, for many things check them and stand in their way. But intelligence and reason can make their way through everything that opposes them, in the manner that nature has formed them and as they choose. Set before your eyes the ease with which reason will be carried through all things, as fire is carried upward, as a stone falls downward, as a cylinder rolls down a slope, and seek for nothing further. All other obstacles either affect the body alone, which is a dead thing, or else they neither crush nor harm us in any way, except through our opinion and the surrender of reason itself. If they did, the person who felt them would at once become bad. Now, with everything else that has a particular constitution, whatever harm befalls it makes the thing that suffers it worse. But in a similar case a human being becomes, if one may put it so, both better and more worthy of praise by making right use of such accidents. Finally, remember that nothing harms one who is truly a citizen unless it harms the state, and nothing harms the state unless it harms law, that is, order. And of the things called misfortunes, not one harms law. What does not harm law, then, harms neither state nor citizen.

34\. For someone steeped in true principles, even the briefest and most ordinary saying is enough to remind him to be free of grief and fear. For example, these lines of Homer:

"Leaves, some the wind scatters on the ground—
So is the race of men."

Your children, too, are leaves. Leaves also are the people who cry out as though they deserved to be believed and bestow their praise, or who curse instead, or blame and sneer in secret. And leaves, in the same way, are those who will receive a man's fame and pass it on to later ages. For all such things "come forth in the season of spring," as the poet says; then the wind throws them down; then the forest puts out other leaves in their place. A brief existence is common to all things, and yet you shun and pursue everything as though it would last forever. A little while, and you will close your eyes; and soon another will mourn the one who carried you to your grave.

35\. A healthy eye ought to see everything visible and not say, "I want only green things," for that is the condition of a diseased eye. Healthy hearing and a healthy sense of smell ought to be ready to perceive everything that can be heard and smelled. A healthy stomach ought to be ready for all food, just as a mill is ready for everything it was made to grind. Accordingly, a healthy understanding ought to be prepared for everything that happens. But the understanding that says, "Let my dear children live, and let everyone praise whatever I do," is an eye that seeks green things, or teeth that seek soft things.

36\. No one is so fortunate that, when he is dying, there will not be some standing by who are pleased at what is about to happen. Suppose he was a good and wise man. Will there not be at least one person to say to himself, "At last we can breathe freely, relieved of this schoolmaster. It is true that he was harsh to none of us, but I sensed that he silently condemned us"? This is what is said of a good man. But in our own case, how many other reasons are there for which many people would like to be rid of us? Consider this, then, when you are dying, and you will depart more contentedly by reflecting as follows: "I am leaving a life in which even my companions, on whose behalf I have struggled so much, prayed so much, and cared so much, themselves wish me gone, hoping perhaps to gain some small advantage by it. Why, then, should anyone cling to a longer stay here?" Do not, however, go away less kindly disposed toward them for this reason. Keep your own character: friendly, benevolent, and mild. Nor should you leave as though you were being torn away. Just as in a quiet death the poor soul separates easily from the body, so your departure from other people ought to be. Nature joined you to them and made you their companion. Does she now dissolve the union? Very well: I am parted from them as from kinsmen, yet not dragged away resisting, but without compulsion, for this too is one of the things in keeping with nature.

37\. Accustom yourself, as far as possible, whenever anyone does anything, to ask yourself: For what purpose is this man doing this? But begin with yourself, and examine yourself first.

38\. Remember that what pulls the strings is the thing hidden within. This is the power of persuasion; this is life; this, if one may say so, is the human being. When you contemplate yourself, never include the vessel that surrounds you or the instruments attached to it. They are like an axe, differing only in that they grow on the body. For these parts are of no more use without the cause that moves and restrains them than the weaver's shuttle without the weaver, the writer's pen without the writer, or the driver's whip without the driver.

## Book XI

1\. These are the properties of the rational soul. It sees itself, analyzes itself, and makes itself whatever it chooses. It enjoys the fruit it bears, whereas the fruits of plants, and whatever in animals corresponds to fruit, are enjoyed by others. It reaches its own end wherever the limit of life may be set. It is not like a dance or a play or anything of the kind, where the whole performance is left incomplete if something cuts it short. In every part, and wherever it may be stopped, the rational soul makes what was set before it full and complete, so that it can say, "I have what is my own." Further, it ranges over the whole universe and the empty space around it, and surveys its form. It reaches out into the infinity of time, and embraces and comprehends the periodic renewal of all things. It understands that those who come after us will see nothing new, and that those before us saw nothing more than we do. In a sense, a man of forty, if he has any understanding at all, has seen, by virtue of the uniformity that prevails, all things that have been and all that will be. This too is a property of the rational soul: love of one's neighbor, truth, modesty, and valuing nothing more than itself, which is also the property of Law. Thus right reason differs not at all from the reason of justice.

2\. You will think little of pleasing songs, of dancing, and of the pancratium, that brutal contest combining boxing and wrestling, if you break the melody of a voice down into its individual sounds and ask yourself of each one whether it masters you. Shame will keep you from admitting that it does. Do the same with dancing, taking each movement and posture separately, and likewise with the pancratium. In all things, then, except virtue and the acts of virtue, remember to turn your attention to their separate parts, and through this division come to value them little. Apply this rule to your whole life as well.

3\. What a soul it is that stands ready, if at any moment it must be separated from the body, to be extinguished, or scattered, or to continue to exist! But this readiness must come from a person's own judgment, not from sheer obstinacy, as with the Christians. It must be considered and dignified, able to persuade another, and free of theatrical display.

4\. Have I done something for the common good? Then I have had my reward. Let this always be present to your mind, and never stop doing such good.

5\. What is your art? To be good. And how is this to be achieved well, except through general principles, some about the nature of the universe and others about the proper constitution of a human being?

6\. Tragedies were first brought onto the stage to remind people of the things that happen to them, to show that it is according to nature for things to happen so, and to teach that if you take pleasure in what is shown on the stage, you should not be troubled by what takes place on the larger stage of life. For you see that these things must come about in this way, and that even those who cry out "O Cithaeron!" must bear them, as Oedipus does in Sophocles, calling on the mountain where he was abandoned as an infant. Indeed, the dramatic poets say some things well, and the following especially:

"If the gods neglect me and my children,
This too has its reason."

And again:

"We must not chafe and fret at what happens."

And:

"Reap life's harvest like the wheat's fruitful ear."

After tragedy came the Old Comedy, which had a commanding freedom of speech and, by its very bluntness, was useful in reminding people to beware of arrogance. For this purpose, too, Diogenes used to borrow from these writers.

But as for the Middle Comedy, which came next, observe what it was; and again, consider for what purpose the New Comedy was introduced, which gradually sank into mere artful imitation. Everyone knows that even these writers say some good things. But the whole design of such poetry and drama: to what end does it look?

7\. How plainly it appears that no other condition of life is so well suited to philosophy as the one in which you now happen to find yourself.

8\. A branch cut off from the branch next to it is necessarily cut off from the whole tree as well. In the same way, a man separated from another man has fallen away from the whole social community. Now, a branch is cut off by someone else. But a man separates himself from his neighbor by his own act when he hates him and turns away from him, and he does not realize that he has at the same time cut himself off from the whole social system. Yet he certainly has this privilege from Zeus, who framed society: it is in our power to grow back onto what is next to us, and once more to become a part that helps make up the whole. If this kind of separation happens often, however, it becomes difficult for the part that detaches itself to be brought back into unity and restored to its former condition. Finally, the branch that grew with the tree from the beginning and has continued to share one life with it is not like the branch that is grafted back on after being cut off. Of the grafted branch, gardeners say something like this: it grows together with the rest of the tree, but it is not of the same mind with it.

9\. Those who try to stand in your way as you proceed according to right reason will not be able to turn you from your proper action. In the same way, do not let them drive you from your kindly feelings toward them. Be on guard equally in both respects: not only in steadiness of judgment and action, but also in gentleness toward those who try to hinder or otherwise trouble you. For it is a weakness to be vexed with them, just as it is a weakness to be diverted from your course of action and give way through fear. Both are equally deserters from their post: the one who yields through fear, and the one who becomes estranged from someone who is by nature his kinsman and friend.

10\. No nature is inferior to art, for the arts imitate the natures of things. If this is so, then the most perfect and most comprehensive of all natures cannot fall short of the skill of art. Now, all the arts do the lesser things for the sake of the greater, and therefore universal nature does so too. Here, indeed, is the origin of justice, and in justice the other virtues have their foundation. For justice will not be observed if we care for middle things, the things indifferent, or if we are easily deceived, careless, and changeable.

11\. The things whose pursuit and avoidance disturb you do not come to you; rather, in a sense, you go to them. Let your judgment about them be at rest, then, and they will remain quiet, and you will not be seen either pursuing or avoiding them.

12\. The soul keeps its spherical form when it neither stretches out toward any object nor contracts inward, neither scatters nor sinks down, but is illuminated by the light by which it sees the truth: the truth of all things and the truth within itself.

13\. Suppose someone despises me. That is his concern. Mine is to make sure I am not found doing or saying anything that deserves contempt. Will someone hate me? That is his concern. But I will be mild and kindly toward everyone, and ready to show even that person his mistake, not reproachfully, nor as if making a display of my patience, but nobly and honestly, like the great Athenian statesman Phocion, unless indeed he was only pretending. For one's inner self ought to be like this, and a person ought to be seen by the gods neither dissatisfied with anything nor complaining. What evil is it to you if you are now doing what agrees with your own nature, and are content with what at this moment suits the nature of the universe, since you are a human being stationed at your post so that what serves the common good may be accomplished in some way?

14\. Men despise one another and flatter one another; and men wish to rise above one another, and yet they grovel before one another.

15\. How rotten and insincere is the person who announces, "I have decided to deal fairly with you!" What are you doing, man? There is no need to give notice of this. It will soon reveal itself in your actions. What you would say ought to be written plainly on your forehead. A person's character shows at once in the eyes, just as the beloved reads everything at a glance in the eyes of a lover. The honest and good person should be exactly like someone with a strong smell, so that anyone who comes near catches the scent whether he wants to or not. But a show of simplicity is like the crooked stick of the proverb, which can never be made straight. Nothing is more shameful than the friendship of the wolf for the sheep in the fable, a false friendship. Avoid this above all. The good, the simple, and the kindly show all these qualities in their eyes, and there is no mistaking them.

16\. The power to live in the best way lies in the soul, provided it is indifferent to things that are indifferent. And it will be indifferent if it looks at each of these things both singly and as a whole, and if it remembers that none of them produces in us any opinion about itself, nor comes to us. These things stay where they are, motionless, and it is we who form judgments about them and, so to speak, write those judgments on ourselves. Yet it is in our power not to write them, and in our power, if such judgments have slipped unnoticed into our minds, to wipe them away. The soul should remember, too, that this vigilance will be needed only for a short time, and then life will be over. Besides, what is so hard about doing this? If these things accord with nature, rejoice in them, and they will come easily to you. But if they run contrary to nature, seek what accords with your own nature and strive toward it, even if it brings you no glory, for everyone is permitted to seek his own good.

17\. Consider where each thing has come from, what it is made of, what it changes into, what it will be once it has changed, and that it will suffer no harm in the process.

18\. If anyone has offended you, consider first: What is my relation to other people? We are made for one another, and in another respect I was made to be set over them, as a ram is set over the flock or a bull over the herd. But examine the matter from first principles, starting here. If all things are not mere atoms, then it is nature that orders everything. And if so, the lower things exist for the sake of the higher, and the higher for the sake of one another.

Second, consider what sort of people they are at table, in bed, and so on. Consider especially what compulsions their opinions place them under, and, as for their acts, the pride with which they do what they do.

Third, if people are acting rightly, we ought not to be displeased. If they are not, it is clear that they act involuntarily and out of ignorance. For just as every soul is deprived of the truth against its will, so it is deprived against its will of the power to treat each person as he deserves. That is why people are pained when they are called unjust, ungrateful, or greedy, or, in a word, wrongdoers to their neighbors.

Fourth, consider that you too do many things wrong, and that you are a man like others. Even if you refrain from certain faults, you still have the inclination to commit them, though you hold back out of cowardice, or concern for your reputation, or some other petty motive.

Fifth, consider that you do not even know for certain whether people are doing wrong, for many things are done with reference to circumstances. In short, a person must learn a great deal before he can pass a sound judgment on another's acts.

Sixth, when you are deeply vexed or grieved, consider that human life lasts only a moment, and that before long we are all laid out dead.

Seventh, it is not people's acts that disturb us, for those acts have their source in the other person's own ruling principle. What disturbs us is our own opinions. Take away those opinions, then. Resolve to let go of your judgment that the act is something terrible, and your anger is gone. How am I to take away these opinions? By reflecting that no wrong done by another brings disgrace on you. For unless the shameful is the only evil, you too must inevitably do many wrongs and become a robber and everything else.

Eighth, consider how much more pain we suffer from the anger and vexation such acts provoke than from the acts themselves that anger and vex us.

Ninth, consider that a kind disposition is invincible, so long as it is genuine and not a forced smile or a part being played. For what can the most violent man do to you if you remain kind toward him, and if, when the chance arises, you gently admonish him and calmly correct his errors at the very moment he is trying to harm you, saying: "No, my child. We were made by nature for something else. I shall certainly not be harmed. It is yourself you are harming, my child." Show him, tactfully and by general principles, that this is so, and that not even bees behave as he does, nor any of the creatures nature has made to live in communities. And you must do this without any double meaning and without reproach, but affectionately, with no rancor in your soul. Do not do it as though you were lecturing him, nor so that some bystander may admire you, but either when he is alone, or, if others are present . . .

Remember these nine rules as if you had received them as a gift from the Muses, and begin at last to be a human being while you are still alive. But you must equally avoid flattering people and being vexed with them, for both are unsociable and lead to harm. In the heat of anger, keep this truth before you: to be swept along by passion is not manly. Mildness and gentleness, being more in keeping with human nature, are also more manly. The person who has these qualities has strength, sinew, and courage, and the person subject to fits of passion and discontent does not. For the nearer a mind comes to freedom from passion, the nearer it comes to strength. And just as the sense of pain is a mark of weakness, so is anger. Whoever yields to pain and whoever yields to anger have both been wounded, and both have surrendered.

And if you like, accept a tenth gift as well, this one from Apollo, the leader of the Muses. It is this: to expect bad people not to do wrong is madness, for whoever expects it wants the impossible. But to allow them to behave that way toward others while expecting them to do you no wrong is unreasonable and tyrannical.

19\. There are four principal ways in which the higher faculty goes astray, and you should guard against them constantly. Whenever you detect one, wipe it out, and say on each occasion: "This thought is unnecessary." "This one tends to break the bonds of society." "What you are about to say does not come from your real thoughts," and you should count it among the most absurd of things for a person not to speak from his real thoughts. The fourth is when you reproach yourself for anything, for this is evidence that the diviner part within you has been overpowered and has given way to the less honorable and perishable part, the body, and to its gross pleasures.

20\. The airy part of you and all the fiery parts mingled in you naturally tend upward. Even so, in obedience to the order of the universe, they are held down here in this compound, the body. Likewise all the earthy and watery parts of you, though they tend downward, are raised up and occupy a position that is not natural to them. In this way even the elements obey the whole. Once they have been placed somewhere, they stay there of necessity until the universe sounds the signal for their dissolution. Is it not strange, then, that your intelligent part alone should be disobedient and discontented with its place? And yet no force is imposed on it, only what accords with its nature. Still it does not submit, but moves in the opposite direction. For any movement toward injustice, intemperance, anger, grief, or fear is simply the act of one who strays from nature. And when the ruling faculty is discontented with anything that happens, it likewise deserts its post, for it was made for piety and reverence toward the gods no less than for justice. These qualities, too, fall under the general heading of contentment with the order of things, and indeed they are prior to acts of justice.

21\. Whoever does not have one and the same aim in life cannot remain one and the same person throughout life. But this is not enough unless one adds what that aim ought to be. People do not agree about all the things the majority in one way or another regard as good, but only about certain things, namely those that concern the common interest. In the same way, we ought to set ourselves an aim that is shared and civic. Whoever directs all his efforts toward this aim will make all his actions consistent, and so will always be the same.

22\. Think of the country mouse and the town mouse, and of the town mouse's alarm and trembling: the old tale, told by Horace, in which the town mouse's rich feast is broken up by a sudden alarm, and the country mouse goes home content with his plain fare.

23\. Socrates used to call the opinions of the many Lamiae, bogeys to frighten children.

24\. At their public spectacles the Spartans set out seats in the shade for strangers, but sat down anywhere themselves.

25\. Socrates excused himself to Perdiccas for not visiting him, saying, "It is so that I may not perish by the worst of all deaths," that is, by receiving a favor and being unable to repay it.

26\. The writings of the Ephesians contained this precept: always keep in mind some one of the people of former times who practiced virtue.

27\. The Pythagoreans tell us to look at the sky in the morning, to remind ourselves of those bodies that always do the same things and perform their work in the same way, and to remind ourselves of their purity and nakedness. For no star wears a veil.

28\. Consider what sort of man Socrates was when he wrapped himself in an animal skin after Xanthippe had taken his cloak and gone out, and what he said to his friends who were ashamed of him and drew back when they saw him dressed that way.

29\. In writing and in reading, you cannot lay down rules for others until you have first learned to follow rules yourself. This is far more true in life.

30\. You are a slave: free speech is not for you.

31\. "And my heart laughed within me." —Homer, *Odyssey*

32\. "And they will curse virtue, speaking harsh words." —Hesiod, *Works and Days*

33\. To look for a fig in winter is a madman's act, and so is looking for your child when that is no longer granted to you. —Epictetus

34\. When a man kisses his child, said Epictetus, he should whisper to himself, "Tomorrow, perhaps, you will die." "But those are words of ill omen." "No word is ill-omened," said Epictetus, "that expresses a work of nature. If it is, then it is ill-omened to speak of ears of grain being reaped."

35\. The unripe grape, the ripe cluster, the dried raisin: all are changes, not into nothing, but into something that does not yet exist. —Epictetus

36\. No one can rob us of our free will. —Epictetus

37\. Epictetus also said that a person must find an art, a set of rules, for giving assent. As for his impulses to act, he must take care that they take account of circumstances, that they serve the common good, and that they are proportioned to the worth of their object. He should keep entirely away from sensual desire, and should feel aversion toward none of the things that are not in our power.

38\. The dispute, then, he said, is not over some ordinary matter, but over whether we are to be mad or sane.

39\. Socrates used to say: "What do you want, the souls of rational beings or of irrational ones?" "Of rational beings." "Of which rational beings, sound or unsound?" "Sound." "Then why do you not seek them?" "Because we have them." "Then why do you fight and quarrel?"

## Book XII

1\. All the things you hope to reach by a roundabout road you can have now, if you do not deny them to yourself. That means disregarding the whole of the past, entrusting the future to providence, and directing the present alone in accordance with piety and justice. In accordance with piety, so that you may be content with the lot assigned to you, for nature made it for you and you for it. In accordance with justice, so that you may always speak the truth freely and without disguise, and do what accords with law and with the worth of each person. Let nothing hinder you: not another's wickedness, nor opinion, nor talk, nor the sensations of the poor flesh that has grown around you, for the part that suffers them will see to that. Whenever the time of your departure draws near, set everything else aside and honor only your ruling faculty and the divinity within you. If you are afraid, not because you must someday stop living, but because you may never have begun to live in accordance with nature, then you will be a human being worthy of the universe that produced you. You will cease to be a stranger in your own country, cease to marvel at everyday events as if they were unexpected, and cease to depend on this or that.

2\. God sees the minds of all people, their ruling principles, stripped of their material covering, their husks and impurities. With his intellect alone he touches only that intelligence which has flowed out of himself into these bodies. If you train yourself to do the same, you will rid yourself of most of your troubles. For whoever disregards the poor flesh that wraps him will surely not trouble himself over clothing, housing, fame, and similar externals and show.

3\. You are made of three things: a little body, a little breath of life, and intelligence. The first two are yours insofar as it is your duty to care for them, but only the third is truly yours. Separate from yourself, that is, from your understanding, whatever others do or say, whatever you yourself have done or said, whatever future things trouble you because they might happen, whatever clings to you against your will in the body that wraps you or in the breath of life that nature has joined to it, and whatever the whirling current outside you sweeps around. Then the intellect, exempt from the things of fate, can live pure and free on its own, doing what is just, accepting what happens, and speaking the truth. If, I say, you separate from this ruling faculty whatever attaches to it through the impressions of the senses, along with things to come and things past, and make yourself like the sphere of Empedocles, in which all the elements rest united,

"All round, and in its joyous rest reposing";

and if you strive to live only the life that is truly yours, which is the present, then you will be able to spend the rest of your life, up to your death, free from turmoil, nobly, and in obedience to your own daemon, the god within you.

4\. I have often wondered how it is that every person loves himself more than all others, yet values his own opinion of himself less than the opinion others hold of him. If a god or a wise teacher were to appear and order someone to think and plan nothing that he would not speak aloud the moment he conceived it, he could not bear it for a single day. So much more do we care about what our neighbors will think of us than about what we think of ourselves.

5\. How can it be that the gods, having arranged all things well and kindly for humankind, overlooked this one thing: that some people, and very good people, who have had, so to speak, the closest communion with the divine and through pious acts and religious observances have been most intimate with it, should never exist again once they have died, but be utterly extinguished?

But if this is so, be assured that if it ought to have been otherwise, the gods would have made it so. For if it were just, it would also be possible, and if it were in accordance with nature, nature would have brought it about. Because it is not so, if indeed it is not so, be convinced that it ought not to have been so. You can see for yourself that in pressing this question you are disputing with the deity. And we would not dispute with the gods in this way unless they were supremely good and just. But if they are, they would not have allowed anything in the ordering of the universe to be neglected unjustly or irrationally.

6\. Practice even the things you despair of mastering. The left hand, clumsy at everything else for lack of practice, grips the reins more firmly than the right, because it has been practiced at this.

7\. Consider what state of body and soul a person should be in when death overtakes him. Consider the shortness of life, the boundless gulf of time past and time to come, and the frailty of all matter.

8\. Contemplate the formative principles of things stripped of their coverings; the purposes of actions; what pain is, what pleasure is, what death and fame are; who is himself the cause of his own unease; how no one is hindered by another; and that everything is a matter of opinion.

9\. In applying your principles, you must be like the pancratiast, not like the gladiator. The pancratiast fights in the all-out contest of boxing and wrestling, where his only weapons are his own hands. The gladiator drops the sword he fights with and is killed; the pancratiast always has his hand with him and needs to do nothing but use it.

10\. See what things are in themselves, analyzing each into its matter, its form, and its purpose.

11\. What power a human being has: to do nothing except what God will approve, and to accept everything that God may give him.

12\. As for whatever happens in accordance with nature, we should blame neither the gods, for they do no wrong, whether willingly or unwillingly, nor human beings, for they do no wrong except unwillingly. It follows that we should blame no one.

13\. How absurd, and how much a stranger to the world, is the person who is surprised by anything that happens in life.

14\. Either there is a fated necessity and an unbreakable order, or a benevolent providence, or a chaos without purpose and without anyone directing it. If there is an unbreakable necessity, why do you resist it? If there is a providence that can be moved by prayer, make yourself worthy of divine help. And if there is a chaos with no one to govern it, be content that in such a storm you have within yourself a guiding intelligence. Even if the storm sweeps you away, let it take the poor flesh, the poor breath, and everything else; the intelligence, at least, it will not carry off.

15\. Does the light of a lamp shine without losing its brilliance until the moment it is put out? And shall the truth within you, and your justice and self-control, be extinguished before you are?

16\. When someone appears to have done wrong, ask yourself: How do I actually know that this is a wrongful act? And even if he has done wrong, how do I know that he has not already condemned himself, which is like tearing at his own face? Consider that wishing a bad person not to do wrong is like wishing that the fig tree would not produce juice in its figs, that infants would not cry, that the horse would not neigh, or that anything else that must happen would not happen. For what else can a person of that character do? If, then, you are irritable, cure this man's disposition.

17\. If it is not right, do not do it; if it is not true, do not say it. For let your efforts be—

18\. In every case, observe what it is that produces an impression in you, and analyze it by dividing it into its form, its matter, its purpose, and the span of time within which it must come to an end.

19\. Realize at last that you have within you something better and more divine than the things that stir up your passions and jerk you about like a puppet on strings. What is in my mind right now? Is it fear, or suspicion, or desire, or something of that kind?

20\. First, do nothing rashly or without a purpose. Second, let your actions aim at nothing other than the common good.

21\. Consider that before long you will be no one and nowhere, and that none of the things you now see will exist, nor any of the people now living. For all things are made by nature to change, to be transformed, and to perish, so that other things may come into being in continuous succession.

22\. Consider that everything is opinion, and that opinion is in your power. Remove your opinion, then, whenever you choose. Like a sailor who has rounded the headland into sheltered water, you will find calm, everything steady, and a bay without a wave.

23\. Any single activity, whatever it may be, suffers no harm from ceasing when its proper time has come, nor does the person who performed it suffer any harm because the act has ended. In the same way, the whole made up of all our acts, which is our life, suffers no harm from ceasing at its proper time, and the person who brings this series to an end at the proper time has not been badly treated. The proper time and the limit are fixed by nature: sometimes by a person's own particular nature, as in old age, but always by universal nature, through the changing of whose parts the whole universe remains forever young and perfect. And whatever benefits the whole is always good and always in season. The end of life, therefore, is no evil for anyone. It is not shameful, since it does not depend on our will and does not run counter to the common interest; and it is good, since it comes in its season, benefits the whole, and is in harmony with it. For in this way a person is moved by God when he moves in the same way as God and when his mind moves toward the same ends.

24\. Keep these three principles ready at hand. First, in what you do, act neither carelessly nor otherwise than justice itself would act; and with regard to whatever may happen to you from outside, consider that it comes either by chance or by providence, and you must neither blame chance nor accuse providence. Second, consider what every being is from its seed until it receives a soul, and from its receiving a soul until it gives that soul back; and consider what things each being is composed of and into what things it is dissolved. Third, imagine that you were suddenly lifted above the earth and could look down on human affairs and see how vast their variety is, while also taking in at a glance the multitude of beings who dwell all around in the air and in the ether, the pure upper region of the heavens. Consider that however often you were lifted up, you would see the same things: sameness of form and brevity of duration. Are these things to be proud of?

25\. Cast away opinion, and you are saved. Who, then, prevents you from casting it away?

26\. When you are troubled about anything, you have forgotten that all things happen according to universal nature. You have forgotten that another person's wrongdoing is nothing to you. You have forgotten, further, that everything that happens has always happened this way, will always happen this way, and is happening this way now, everywhere. You have forgotten, too, how close the kinship is between a person and the whole human race, for it is a community not of a little blood or seed, but of intelligence. You have forgotten that every person's intelligence is a god and flows out from the Deity. You have forgotten that nothing is truly anyone's own, but that a person's child, his body, and his very soul came from the Deity. You have forgotten that everything is opinion. And lastly, you have forgotten that each of us lives only in the present moment, and that this is all anyone loses.

27\. Constantly call to mind those who complained bitterly about something, and those who were most conspicuous for their great fame, their misfortunes, their enmities, or their fortunes of any kind; then ask where they all are now. Smoke and ash and a story, or not even a story. Keep before your mind every example of this sort: how Fabius Catellinus lived in the country, Lucius Lupus in his gardens, Stertinius at Briae, Tiberius on Capreae, and Velius Rufus, or Rufus at Velia. In short, think of every eager pursuit bound up with arrogance, and how worthless everything is that people strain after so violently; and how much more philosophical it is, in whatever opportunities come your way, to show yourself just, self-controlled, and obedient to the gods, and to do so with complete simplicity. For the pride that takes pride in its own lack of pride is the most insufferable of all.

28\. To those who ask, "Where have you seen the gods? How do you know that they exist, so that you worship them?" I answer, first, that they may be seen even with the eyes; and second, that I have never seen my own soul either, and yet I honor it. So it is with the gods: from what I constantly experience of their power, I know that they exist, and I revere them.

29\. The security of life lies in this: to examine everything thoroughly, seeing what it is in itself, what its matter is and what its form; and with your whole soul to do what is just and to speak the truth. What remains, then, but to enjoy life by linking one good thing to another, so as not to leave even the smallest gap between them?

30\. There is one light of the sun, though it is broken up by walls, mountains, and countless other things. There is one common substance, though it is distributed among countless bodies, each with its own qualities. There is one soul, though it is distributed among infinite natures and individual beings. There is one intelligent soul, though it seems to be divided. Now, among the things just mentioned, all the other parts, such as those that are air and matter, are without sensation and have no fellowship with one another; and yet even these are held together by the intelligent principle and by the gravitation that draws them toward the same place. But intellect, in a way peculiar to itself, tends toward whatever is of its own kind and unites with it, and its feeling for communion is never broken.

31\. What do you want? To go on existing? Do you want, then, to have sensation, movement, and growth, and then to stop growing; to use your speech; to think? Which of all these things seems to you worth desiring? But if it is easy to set little value on all of them, turn to what remains, which is to follow reason and God. Yet it is inconsistent with honoring reason and God to be troubled that death will deprive a person of those other things.

32\. How small a fraction of boundless and unfathomable time is allotted to each of us, for it is very soon swallowed up in eternity! How small a part of the whole substance, how small a part of the universal soul; and on how small a clod of the whole earth you crawl! Reflecting on all this, consider nothing great except to act as your nature leads you, and to endure what the common nature brings.

33\. How does the ruling faculty make use of itself? Everything depends on this. All the rest, whether within the power of your will or not, is only lifeless ash and smoke.

34\. The reflection best suited to make us despise death is this: even those who held pleasure to be a good and pain an evil have nonetheless despised death.

35\. Consider the person for whom only what comes in its due season is good, for whom it makes no difference whether he has performed more or fewer acts in keeping with right reason, and for whom it is all the same whether he looks upon the world for a longer or a shorter time. For such a person, death holds no terror.

36\. Man, you have been a citizen of this great state, the world. What difference does it make to you whether it was for five years or for three? For what accords with the laws is just for everyone alike. Where, then, is the hardship, if it is neither a tyrant nor an unjust judge who sends you out of the state, but nature, who brought you into it? It is as if a praetor, the magistrate responsible for staging the public shows, had hired an actor and then dismissed him from the stage. "But I have not played all five acts, only three." Well said; but in life, three acts make the whole play. For what counts as a complete drama is decided by the one who was once the cause of its composition and is now the cause of its dissolution; and you are the cause of neither. Depart, then, contented, for the one who releases you is contented too.
